Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) excel at generating human-like responses but often struggle
with interactive tasks that require access to real-time information. This limitation poses
challenges in finance, where models must access up-to-date information, such as recent news
or price movements, to support decision-making. To address this, we introduce Financial
Agent, a knowledge-grounding approach for LLMs to handle financial queries using real-
time text and tabular data. Our contributions are threefold: First, we develop a Financial
Context Dataset of over 50,000 financial queries paired with the required context. Second,
we develop FinBloom 7B, a custom 7 billion parameter LLM, by fine-tuning Bloom 7B
on 14 million financial news articles from Reuters and Deutsche Presse-Agentur (DPA),
alongside a random sample of 25% from 12 million Securities and Exchange Commission
(SEC) filings. Third, we fine-tune FinBloom 7B using the Financial Context Dataset
to serve as a Financial Agent. This agent generates relevant financial context, enabling
efficient real-time data retrieval to answer user queries. By reducing latency and eliminating
the need for users to manually provide accurate data, our approach significantly enhances
the capability of LLMs to handle dynamic financial tasks. Our proposed approach makes
real-time financial decisions, algorithmic trading and other related tasks streamlined, and
is valuable in contexts with high-velocity data flows.
1. Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) use deep learning techniques to learn from massive text
datasets to solve a variety of tasks using natural language (Devlin et al., 2018; Brown et al.,
2020; Scao et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). The most common tasks where these models have
shown their proficiency include natural language generation, question-answering, reasoning,
translation, summarization, etc. As the general-purpose models grow in size and learn from

a larger data corpus, we observe that they become more versatile and develop the ability to
handle multiple tasks that previously required separate specialized models. However, areas
such as finance, healthcare, science, law, among others, often have their own domain-specific
vocabulary that requires the fine-tuning of general-purpose models to perform domain-
specific tasks (Wu et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2022; Taylor et al., 2022). As LLMs get larger, it
will be interesting to see whether general-purpose LLMs can work equally well as domain-
specific LLMs simply with appropriate prompts. A rather more urgent problem that needs
attention is that many of the domains require access to real-time data arising from several
sources to handle various tasks with high accuracy and efficiency, which is currently beyond
the capability of frozen LLMs that are not grounded in real-time knowledge and work as
static systems as shown in Figure 1. For instance, in the context of finance, a user is likely
to prefer using an LLM that is knowledge-grounded with real-time news and asset prices
as they would want to make decisions based on accurate financial news and tabular data
related to their query. Unless one uses a financial LLM for educational purposes, it will be
of limited use, as most of its responses will be based on stale data that is of little value to
investors or analysts. However, there are innumerable challenges when it comes to training
or fine-tuning an LLM on real-time data that we list below:
1. Training or fine-tuning an LLM is very costly: Financial data such as asset prices
change with a high frequency and similarly news data with an ability to impact asset
prices is generated continuously in various parts of the world. Training or fine-tuning
LLMs require an update in the model parameters which is quite time-consuming.
Even if one attempts a small duration of fine-tuning based on batches of most recent
numeric and text data, the LLM will still remain outdated as financial data flows at
high velocity. Unless LLMs have the capability of quick online learning, knowledge-
grounding based on frequent training or fine-tuning will not entirely help.
2. Lack of knowledge on what the LLM has learned: Most LLMs learn from massive
data corpus, but as users of the system, we do not know what the model has learned
and where it may commit errors. Only intensive testing of the models often reveal
its weakness. Therefore, without a complete knowledge that LLM has learned from
the new data appropriately, one may not put it to use in financial contexts where
decisions have to be made based on new data.
Handling the above challenges would be useful for any general-purpose or domain-specific
LLM; therefore, there is a large body of recent research on knowledge-grounding of LLMs
(Li et al., 2021; Peng et al., 2023; Carta et al., 2023). An alternative approach to solve
the above problem is to keep that LLM frozen, that is, do not change or fine-tune the
weights of the LLM, rather add modules to the LLM for knowledge-grounding. Zhang
et al. (2024) implemented this method of knowledge grounding in the financial domain,
however, their approach focuses specifically on making trading decisions by leveraging LLMs
to capitalize on market intelligence and technical indicators. Our study takes a distinct
approach by designing and implementing modules that enable retrieval and incorporation
of pertinent financial data for answering any financial query. This data augmentation is
intended to enhance the LLM’s capacity to respond accurately to a diverse array of financial
inquiries. The first module is the Data Module that provides access to real-time information

dynamic text and tabular data streams. Our methodology leverages an external agent com-

contextual information required by a larger LLM (ChatGPT) for accurate query resolution.

1. We develop a custom Financial Context Dataset 1, containing over 50,000 financial

2. We fine-tune a 7 billion parameter LLM, referred to as FinBloom 7B 2, on over 14

Existing approaches to knowledge-grounding in LLMs follow different methodologies,
which have a number of similarities, but are known in the literature with different ter-
minologies. We address these approaches in the later part of the paper. We adopt an
agent-based procedure to perform knowledge-grounding keeping in mind that financial data
is high volume and high velocity for which high latency approaches may not be suitable. An
overview of the proposed system—designed to respond efficiently to dynamic, real-time fi-
nancial information—is shown in Figure 2. The pipeline consists of three main components:
a Data Module, a Financial Agent, and an LLM. Because real-time financial information is
typically available in tabular and textual forms, the Data Module maintains two continu-
ously updated repositories: (i) a tabular database containing prices, financial metrics, and
financial statements, and (ii) a text database that stores the latest news. Together, these
repositories provide streaming context that is accessible to the Financial Agent. Given a
user queryx, the Financial Agent first interprets the request and retrieves the most relevant
news and tabular records from the Data Module. The retrieved content is then transformed
into a textual contextcand concatenated with the original query. The resulting input
(c, x) is provided to the LLM, enabling the model to generate an answer grounded in the
retrieved, up-to-date financial data. Details about the Data Module implementation and
retrieval are provided in Appendix A. A detailed example on the working of the proposed
system is provided in Appendix D. Additional examples in Appendix D also highlight how
advanced LLMs may be of limited use to experts if not grounded with the necessary data.

2. Literature Review
In this section, we provide a review on knowledge-grounding, contributions of LLMs in
finance, and popular financial datasets for financial text mining. We also talk about the
limitations of some of the popular knowledge-grounding approaches when applied to the
area of finance.
2.1 Knowledge-Grounding of LLMs
The usual strategy for broadening the applicability of an LLM to a particular downstream
task has conventionally entailed refining the model’s parameters. This involves the process
of training certain or all layers of the LLM on a bespoke dataset tailored to the specific
requirements of the given downstream task (Radford et al., 2019). However, these strategies
exhibit a considerable computational cost, especially if the data is dynamic and the model
has to be updated frequently. The fundamental rationale for knowledge-grounding in LLMs
resides in the motivation to equip these models with the faculties of reasoning and contextual
comprehension based on relevant external data, as opposed to them being regarded as
repositories of static knowledge.
In recent years, a plethora of viable alternatives have emerged, offering potential en-
hancements to the conventional fine-tuning methodology. Wei et al. (2022) introduced
“chain-of-thought” (CoT), a few-shot prompting technique for LLMs. CoT employs se-

quential examples within a prompt, comprising task inputs and intermediate steps, to drive
contextual understanding and reasoning in large models. Brown et al. (2020) demonstrated
GPT-3’s ability to learn complex tasks in a few-shot setting. But few-shot prompting
requires manual effort to design the optimal prompt for any required downstream task.
Reynolds and McDonell (2021) argued that few-shot learning isn’t a method of task learn-
ing but rather a method of task location in the existing space of the model’s learned tasks.
They introduced the concept of metaprompt programming, through which the job of writ-
ing task-specific prompts can be assigned to the LLM itself. Gao et al. (2020) proposed an
improvement in the few-shot strategy, where rather than relying on the arbitrary selection
of random examples and their inclusion in the query, which lacks a guarantee of emphasizing
the most informative demonstrations, their approach involved sequential random sampling
of a single example from each class for every input resulting in the creation of multiple
concise demonstration sets. Shin et al. (2020) introduced AUTOPROMPT, an approach
that automatically constructs prompts by combining primary task inputs with an array
of trigger tokens, adhering to a predetermined template. The set of trigger tokens used
is same for all the inputs and is learned through a specialized adaptation of the gradient-
based search strategy. The composite prompt is then supplied as input to a Mask Language
Model. It was observed that for Natural Language Inference (NLI) tasks, AUTOPROMPT
was comparable to a supervised fine-tuned BERT model.
Another limitation inherent in the few-shot prompting methodology stems from the con-
strained token capacity that the LLMs can intake. This might have thousands of examples
that we need the LLM to learn from. A number of parameter efficient fine-tuning method-
ologies have been proposed to tackle this issue. Liu et al. (2021) proposed P-Tuning, a
6


[Page 7]
technique where the prompt tokens in the input embedding (containing context tokens and
prompt tokens) are treated as pseudo tokens and mapped as trainable embedding tensors.
This continuous prompt is modeled using a prompt encoder consisting of a bidirectional
LSTM and is then optimized using the downstream loss function. Another unique ap-
proach is Prefix Tuning (Li and Liang, 2021), where a sequence of continuous task-specific
vectors are prepended to the input of an autoregressive LM, or to both the encoder and
decoder layers of a Encoder-Decoder Model. The prefix consists of trainable parameters
which do not correspond to real tokens in a model’s embedding. Instead of training the
model’s parameters on the loss function, only the parameters in the prefix are optimized.
A similar methodology was used in Prompt Tuning (Lester et al., 2021), but without any
intermediate-layer prefixes. Dettmers et al. (2024) proposed QLoRA, which introduces a
memory-efficient fine-tuning method for LLMs by quantizing the pre-trained model to 4-
bits and using Low Rank Adapters (LoRA), achieving comparable performance to 16-bit
fine-tuning with reduced memory.
An alternate approach for the knowledge-grounding of LLMs involves equipping them
with a retriever module that can access information relevant to the user’s query from a
database. Lewis et al. (2020) introduced a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) model,
where they integrated a pre-trained retriever module with a pre-trained seq2seq model. The
retriever provides latent documents conditioned on the input, and theseq2seqmodel then
conditions on these latent documents together with the input to generate the output. Dinan
et al. (2018) designed a dialogue model, where within the conversational framework, the
chatbot is equipped with access to a curated collection of passages that maintain relevance
to the ongoing discourse. At each turn, using a standard information retrieval system, the
chatbot retrieves the top 7 articles for the last two turns of conversation. An attention
mechanism is used to perform refined selection of specific sentences that will be used to
create the next response. Izacard et al. (2022) created ATLAS, a RAG Model which is
capable of few-shot learning. Peng et al. (2023) presented LLM-Augmenter, a module which,
in addition to knowledge retrieval and prompt generation, checks the response generated
by a fixed LLM for hallucinations. If the response is not correct, it generates a feedback
message which is used to improve the prompt. This cycle continues until the response by
the LLM is verified. Li et al. (2021) used a three step data cleaning procedure to supply an
LLM with relevant context: retrieving associated triples from a knowledge graph, finding
related triples by computing cosine similarity between the triples and the query, further
refining the choices by estimating the semantic similarity score.
Despite significant advancements in knowledge-grounding of LLMs, efficient deployment
of them for real-time processing of enormous data flowing at high frequency, such as financial
data, remains a challenge.
2.2 Limitations of Traditional RAG Models in Retrieving Financial Data
Recent years have witnessed a surge in the development and application of Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) systems. By combining the power of LLMs with information
retrieval techniques, RAG systems have the potential to revolutionize various industries.
However, despite their promise, these systems still face several limitations that hinder their
widespread adoption, particularly in complex and regulated domains.
7


[Page 8]
A major source of confusion in the literature is that the termRAGis often used broadly
to denote anyretrieval + generationpipeline. In this broader sense, many systems (includ-
ing ours) can be considered RAG-like. In this section, however, we use “traditional RAG”
to specifically refer to the dominant design pattern in which a large corpus is embedded
into a dense vector space and retrieved via approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search
over text chunks. The limitations discussed below target dense-embedding retrieval over
textified content, which is particularly fragile when the underlying knowledge is structured
and fast-flowing financial data.
One of the core challenges in such RAG systems lies in the effectiveness of the retrieval
mechanism. As highlighted by Gupta et al. (2024), while powerful, retrieval can struggle
with ambiguous queries and niche knowledge domains. The reliance on dense vector repre-
sentations can sometimes lead to the retrieval of irrelevant documents, which can negatively
impact the quality of the generated response. Cuconasu et al. (2024) further emphasized this
point, demonstrating that high-scoring retrieved documents that are not directly relevant
to the query can degrade LLM effectiveness. Moreover, the quality of the generated output
can be influenced by the number of retrieved passages, as outlined in Jin et al. (2024); while
increasing the number of passages can initially improve quality, it can eventually lead to a
decline in performance due to distraction and context dilution.
When integrating knowledge graphs (KGs) into RAG systems, additional challenges arise
in effectively utilizing structured information. Agrawal et al. (2024) identified several criti-
cal failure points in existing KG-based RAG methods, including insufficient focus on ques-
tion intent and inadequate context gathering from KG facts. The real-world applications
of retrieval-augmented systems, especially in expert domains, are often characterized by
complex and nuanced requirements. As noted by Zhao et al. (2024), the one-size-fits-all ap-
proach to data augmentation may not be suitable for all scenarios. In compliance-regulated
sectors, systems must also adhere to stringent data privacy, security, and governance re-
quirements; Bruckhaus (2024) highlighted the importance of ensuring that sensitive data is
not exposed or misused during the retrieval and generation process.
Dealing with financial data presents additional challenges to traditional dense-vector
RAG pipelines, primarily because the underlying knowledge is predominantlystructured
(tables, time series, and numeric metrics), while dense-vector RAG is optimized forun-
structured text. Specifically:
1.Difficulties in representing financial data as retrievable text.Financial
data is predominantly stored in structured, tabular formats (spreadsheets, relational
databases, and time-series stores). Traditional dense-vector RAG commonly requires
converting these tables into unstructured text chunks and embedding them for ANN
search. This conversion is often impractical at scale, introduces redundancy, and can
create errors or inconsistencies (e.g., mismatched units, stale snapshots, or fragmented
rows). Further, because tabular structure encodes meaning through schema, keys, and
alignment, naive textification may discard or obscure critical relationships needed for
precise retrieval and reasoning.
2.Retriever precision requirements for financial queries.Financial queries often
specify companies, financial metrics and date ranges. Dense-vector retrieval over
textified tables is not guaranteed to return the exact cell-level values required, and
8


[Page 9]
small retrieval errors can lead to materially incorrect answers. Incomplete retrieval
yields missing evidence, while excessive retrieval burdens the generator with irrelevant
context, amplifying hallucinations and misinterpretations.
3.High-velocity, high-volume updates.Financial datasets are frequently updated
(earnings releases, macroeconomic indicators, market data, filings), and dense-vector
indices require periodic re-embedding and re-indexing to stay current. In high-velocity
settings, this pipeline can become costly and latency-prone, increasing the risk of stale
retrieval and outdated generation.
Motivated by these limitations, our system avoids embedding the structured financial
database into a vector space and does not rely on ANN search over textified tables. Instead,
structured financial retrieval is performed viaschema-aligned query prediction: a small text-
to-text Financial Agent generates a Structured Data Request, which is compiled into exact
data request queries over the relational store, returning precise responses. Only the retrieved
slice is then linearized into text for the LLM to perform explanation and synthesis.
2.3 Financial Datasets Review
The domain of financial language processing has witnessed a significant rise in the devel-
opment of annotated datasets, each serving distinct purposes within the field. Through
Table 1, we have highlighted some prominent examples that have been widely used in the
area of finance for training models. In the next sections, we discuss the core contributions
of the paper in detail. The key contribution is the creation of a Financial Agent that is
capable of understanding the context needed for a user query, extracting the relevant data
from the databases, and supporting an LLM to handle the contextualized query effectively.
3. Financial Context Dataset
For any given user query, a Financial Agent is expected to provide the relevant context,
which includes numeric and text data, which can be appended to the user query for further
processing. In this section, we discuss the first core contribution of the paper, which is the
Financial Context Dataset, consisting of 50,000 samples of user query and its corresponding
context. The proposed dataset can be used to fine-tune a Financial Agent specifically for
context identification and data extraction. In this section, we present a detailed account of
the dataset creation and its description.
To fine-tune our model effectively, the dataset should be diverse in terms of sentence
structure and comprehensive in terms of financial metrics and companies. Specifically,
the dataset should include queries in a variety of forms, such as simple yes/no questions,
comparative questions, and open-ended questions. This will help the model to learn to
understand the different ways that users can present their queries. Additionally, the dataset
should include a large number of financial metrics and companies, in the form of their
many possible names and other ways they are referred to generally. This diversity in the
dataset will enable our model to generalize well to a wide range of user queries, ensuring
that it can accurately identify the required information. To construct this dataset, which
consists of natural language user queries and their corresponding structured data requests,
we followed a multi-method query sampling approach. The first method involved collecting

After creating 50000 queries using a template-based approach, we create two additional
columns in the dataset, namely, “Required Data” and “Structured Data Request” . The
“Required Data” column specifies the necessary information required for answering the
query, comprising company names, their specific metrics required for analysis, and the
corresponding date ranges for data retrieval. Each metric is associated with a list of “Related
Metrics”, which are also to be retrieved from the Data Module. A snippet of our dataset,
3 out of 50000 rows, with all three columns is shown in Table 3. The inclusion of related
metrics serves four primary purposes: (1) It provides additional contextual information
for an efficient processing of the query by an LLM. (2) It enhances the robustness of the
analysis by mitigating potential ambiguities or inaccuracies in the data. Financial metrics
often have complex interdependencies, and providing related metrics ensures that even if
the primary metric is misunderstood, incomplete, or unavailable, the LLM can derive or
approximate insights using the supplementary data. This redundancy in data retrieval
acts as a safeguard against potential errors, thereby improving the reliability of the query
responses. (3) It enables the generation of enriched and multidimensional insights that
go beyond the scope of the original query. By retrieving additional metrics linked to the
primary ones, the system can identify trends, correlations, or anomalies that might not be
apparent when considering the primary metric alone. This capability allows the LLM to
provide more comprehensive, informed, and actionable responses, thereby increasing the
value of the analysis for end-users. (4) By leveraging “Related Metrics”, the LLM can
perform multi-metric calculations (e.g., ratios, averages, or differences), which can be useful

4. FinBloom 7B: A Large Language Model for Finance
Our approach for addressing user queries requires a Financial Agent for forming a structured
request of the financial data that the Data Module can understand and respond to. The
user’s query is typically in natural language, which means that it may not explicitly contain
all of the necessary information, like recent numbers and news, that is needed to answer it.
Hence, the Agent needs to analyze the user’s query and intelligently identify the essential
financial metrics, as well as any related metrics and news, that are needed to answer the
query. It also needs to identify the companies for which the data is required, as well as the
date ranges for which the data is needed. It finally needs to ensure that these requirements
are presented in the form of a data request function that the Data Module can interpret.
Manually specifying a set of hard rules to handle all possible user queries and perform these
tasks would be very tedious and complicated. Therefore, a better approach is to fine-tune
a text-to-text generation model on a large dataset of natural language user queries for
generating the corresponding structured query formats which contain all the information
required to answer the user query. There are quite a few financial LLMs such as FiMA
(Xie et al. (2023)), FinGPT (Yang et al. (2023a)), CFGPT (Li et al. (2023)), InvestLM
(Yang et al. (2023b)) available that can be fine-tuned to serve as an agent for the task of
creating structured data request. However, most of these LLMs exhibit limitations that may
hinder their effectiveness in real-world applications, especially for the purpose of identifying
financial context for a given query.
Many domain-specific models are typically developed by starting with a general-purpose
base model, which is subsequently fine-tuned using human-curated financial datasets (for
example, FinMA (Xie et al. (2023)), InvestLM (Yang et al. (2023b))) such as instruction
datasets, question-answer datasets, structured datasets, or synthetic datasets. To the best
of our knowledge, most existing financial LLMs are not trained on high-quality, large-scale
domain-specific text corpora. This limitation primarily arises from the scarcity of extensive
14


[Page 15]
and ethically 4 usable financial text corpora, as such resources are often difficult to access
or compile from online channels. Many human-curated financial datasets used for training
finance-specific LLMs are of limited size and highly structured; therefore, it may fail to con-
vey domain-specific nuances and may not capture the complex inter-dependencies inherent
in financial metrics and text. Human-curated datasets are also often inherent with human
biases, leading to LLMs with shallow understanding of financial text. Using financial text
corpora, such as large volumes of written financial text (books, news, filings, etc.), enriches
the model’s knowledge of domain-relevant terminology, concepts, and context. Even in
cases where one desires to train domain-specific instruction models, it is advisable to take
a hybrid approach involving training on text-corpora followed by fine-tuning on instruction
dataset. The existing domain-specific LLMs lack this phase of training on financial text
corpora. Some financial LLMs such as FinGPT (Yang et al. (2023a)), CFGPT (Li et al.
(2023)) are trained on publicly available financial text corpora, but are not competitive
when evaluated on financial benchmarks in the later part of this section.
To create the financial LLM, we choose a foundational model with robust general lan-
guage understanding and the potential for domain-specific specialization in finance. We
began by selecting the Bloom 7B parameter model as the base, leveraging its strong lin-
guistic capabilities as a foundation for fine-tuning. To equip the model with financial exper-
tise, we used a large corpus of financial news articles procured from Reuters and Deutsche
Presse-Agentur (DPA). This dataset encompasses a wide array of financial topics, including
market trends, economic indicators, corporate developments, and regulatory changes. The
Reuters dataset spans the period from January 1, 2003, to December 31, 2012, while the
DPA dataset covers the timeframe from June 1, 2001, to May 31, 2011. The combined
dataset consists of over 14 million articles. The high-quality financial data used for train-
ing is expected to lead to an LLM that understands interactions between various financial
metrics and concepts well. We also utilized the publicly available SEC filings data from the
period 31st Mar 2009 to 31st Oct 2023. The SEC filings consisted of overall 12.23 million
documents but we utilized a random sample of 25% for the purpose of fine-tuning to avoid
biasing the model with a large volume of “regulatory reporting data” . The details of the

6. Work Flow of Various Modules
In this section, we describe in detail the functionality of different modules in our framework
and the process of how they interact with each other. Our multi-module system is designed
to process and respond to financial queries efficiently and accurately, leveraging a specialized
Financial Agent and a robust Data Repository. The core of this system is the seamless
communication and collaboration between these modules, ensuring a cohesive and dynamic
flow of information from the initial user query to the final response generated by the LLM.
When a user query is received, it is first handled by the Financial Agent that extracts
relevant information from the Data Module to handle the user query appropriately. The
Data Module serves as the repository for financial data, encompassing tabular financial
information as well as real-time news data pertaining to diverse companies, sectors, and
industries. It operates in a dynamic fashion, regularly ingesting and incorporating fresh
data to ensure that the information it provides remains current and up-to-date. Upon re-
ceiving a data request from the Financial Agent, the Data Module constructs a dataframe
containing pertinent financial data, conforming to the specifications of the data request.
The Data Module provides the relevant news data based on semantic matching of news
items with the user query. We use RoBERTa-based (Liu, 2019) semantic search to detect
similarity between the requested query and the news headlines. We develop FinRoBERTa 5
by adapting the RoBERTa-large transformer model (about 400M parameters) to the finan-
cial domain through fine-tuning. RoBERTa-large is fine-tuned for 2 epochs on a mixture of
financial text sources, namely Reuters, DPA, and SEC data, to strengthen domain-specific
language understanding and also on financial sentiment datasets (Malo et al. (2014); Sinha
et al. (2022)) For evaluation, we compare three models: (i) the base RoBERTa-large, (ii)
FinBERT, and (iii) our domain-adapted FinRoBERTa. All models are tested on the FOMC
dataset (part of FinBen) for three-way stance classification (hawkish,dovish,neutral). Ta-
ble 8 reports the results, demonstrating that FinRoBERTa substantially outperforms both

An illustrative example to demonstrate how the Financial Agent architecture works in
knowledge-grounding of LLMs and helps to answer financial queries comprehensively has
been provided in Example 1 in Appendix D. In summary, we chose the architecture shown in
Figure 6 over alternatives for several key reasons. Although fine-tuning a general-purpose
LLM like GPT-3.5 with financial data is possible, we opted for a domain-specific model
to ensure greater control over training, customization, and deployment costs–crucial for
real-time financial applications–and to enable faster inference. We also deliberately kept
the Data Module separate from the Financial Agent to allow for a modular system de-
sign, ensuring flexibility in data updates without needing to retrain the agent frequently.
Recent updates in general-purpose LLM allow web access. However, it is inadequate for
high-volume, high-velocity financial contexts. These models often lack access to structured
financial data (like intraday trading data, earnings call transcripts, real-time market feeds,
regulatory filings, etc.) and are prone to hallucinations or approximations when precise fig-
ures are needed (like revenue growth, dividend yield, volume spikes, sectoral indices, etc.).

tions illustrated in Examples 2 and 3 in Appendix D. Finally, Example 4 in Appendix D
compares the performance of an LLM with web access against the same LLM operating in

7. Conclusions
This study emphasizes the role of knowledge-grounding in LLMs, particularly for interactive
tasks that require real-time information access. First, we developed the Financial Context
Dataset, which comprises over 50,000 financial queries paired with the contextual informa-
tion necessary for their resolution. Building on this foundation, we trained FinBloom 7B, a
7 billion parameter language model, using a corpus of over 14 million financial news articles
sourced from Reuters and Deutsche Presse-Agentur (DPA) and 12 million documents from
SEC filings. Subsequently, we fine-tuned FinBloom 7B on the Financial Context Dataset to
create a Financial Agent. This agent is capable of generating the required context for user
queries and efficiently retrieving it from a data module to address financial queries. A key
challenge in financial decision-making lies in mitigating the high latency associated with
multiple interactions with large-scale LLMs (e.g., GPT-3.5 or GPT-4). To address this, the
fine-tuned Financial Agent seamlessly integrates with any large-scale LLM. In our proposed
framework, the user submits a query, the Financial Agent processes and retrieves the nec-
essary context, and the LLM subsequently analyzes the query along with the contextual
information. This design ensures low latency, enabling effective handling of high-velocity
financial data effectively.
Future research could expand this framework into a multi-agent system, integrating
additional agents such as financial video analysis tools to further enhance its capabilities.
With these advancements, our work holds the potential to transform the delivery of financial
services, making them more efficient, personalized, and accessible to a broader audience.

Appendix A. Data Module Implementation and Retrieval Details
The Data Module is implemented as a logical abstraction layer that unifies access to two
physically distinct backends optimized for different access patterns: (i) a relational store
for structured/tabular financial information and (ii) a document store with a vector index
for news. The Financial Agent interacts with the Data Module through a single, structured
request interface (cf. the “Structured Data Request” format in Table 3). Internally, the
Data Module routes sub-requests to the appropriate backend, then merges the results into
(a) a tabular result (as apandasDataFrame) and (b) a ranked list of relevant news items.
These outputs are subsequently linearized into the textual contextcused by the language
model (as illustrated in Figure 2).
A.1 Tabular Data Store
Structured financial data (e.g., fundamental metrics, prices, and other numeric series) are
stored in a relational database (PostgreSQL). The schema follows a star design to support
efficient filtering by company, metric, and date range:
•companies(company id, ticker, name, sector, ...)
•metrics(metric id, name, unit, frequency, ...)
•metric values(company id, metric id, date, value, updated at, ...)
This organization supports the dominant query pattern induced by the structured request
representation: retrieving specific metrics for specific companies over a specified date inter-
val.
A.2 News Data Store
News are stored as documents in a single Elasticsearch index that serves as both a document
store and a vector-search backend. Each document contains structured metadata alongside
a dense embedding:
•article id(ID),source(provider),published at(timestamp)
•headline,body
•tickersandsectors(entity tags for filtering)
•embedding: a 768-dimensional dense vector field (stored asdense vector).
Embeddings are pre-computed using a RoBERTa-based encoder Liu (2019) fine-tuned on
financial news, and stored directly in the index to enable ANN retrieval with cosine simi-
larity.
A.3 Update Cadence by Data Type
The Data Module supports different update cadences depending on the data source:
27


[Page 28]
•Market data (prices, volumes).Intraday prices and volumes are ingested in micro-
batches from the data provider; in our deployment this results in an approximate lag
of30 minutesfrom exchange publication to availability within the Data Module.
•Fundamental metrics and statements.Quarterly and annual fundamentals are
ingested viadaily batch jobs, which is sufficient for the decision-support scenarios
targeted in this work.
•News articles.News feeds are polled at short intervals (every 30 minutes); newly
arrived items are tokenized, embedded, and indexed for vector search. It is ensured
that duplicate news items do not get stored.
This describes a research-grade implementation of the logical data layer. An industry de-
ployment can reduce latency further by adopting higher-frequency ingestion and streaming
infrastructure.
For structured metrics, the relational store is treated as append-only, i.e. updates do not
overwrite existing rows. Instead, new records are inserted with anupdated attimestamp.
When answering historical queries (i.e. queries specifying an explicit past interval, for
instance, ‘from Apr 2016 to Jul 2017’ in Query 3, Table 3), the Data Module filters as
per the requested date range or the recent values in case date range is not specified. For
news, the system preserves the full history of articles. Retrieval first applies filters on
published atandtickers/sectors, and only then applies semantic similarity ranking.
The “Structured Data Request” representation encodes: (i) companies, (ii) metrics (and
related metrics), (iii) a date range. When the Financial Agent processes a user query, it
generates the same structured representation. The Data Module then executes the following
steps:
1.Normalize company entities.Company strings are matched to internalcompany ids
using canonical mappings (ticker→company) together with a curated alias list (alter-
native names and abbreviations).
2.Normalize metrics.Metric strings are mapped tometric ids in a metrics catalog
that encodes frequency (daily/quarterly/annual) and the target table. If the request
includes related metrics, these are expanded into additionalmetric ids.
3.Parse dates into explicit intervals.Date ranges are converted into explicit start–
end intervals, enabling consistent filtering of time series. If date range is not provided
in the query, it is assumed that the query is related to the most recent dates.
4.Issue parameterized SQL.For each(company id, metric id)pair, the Data
Module issues parameterized SQL restricted to the requested interval. For quarterly
metrics, values are aligned to the nearest reporting period as described in Section 6.
A.4 News Retrieval via RoBERTa-Based Semantic Search (FinRoBERTa)
News retrieval follows a two-stage pipeline:
1.Embedding model.A RoBERTa-based encoder Liu (2019) is fine-tuned on finan-
cial news headlines (we refer to this as FinRoBERTa) and short snippets using a
28


[Page 29]
contrastive loss objective. The model produces 768-dimensional embeddings for both
queries and headlines.
2.Filtered vector search.All headlines are embedded offline and stored in Elas-
ticsearch as adense vectorfield. At query time, the query is embedded and used
to retrieve the top-knearest neighbours by cosine similarity using approximate kNN
search, subject to filters (ticker, sector and date range constraints) derived from the
structured request.
We tune the retrieval hyperparameters (k= 20 and a cosine similarity thresholdt= 0.2)
on a held-out validation set of (query, relevant-article) pairs. Articles with similarity below
tare discarded to avoid introducing weakly related news into the context. We keep the
number of headlines per query small to control the context length.

Appendix B. Query Collection, Templatization and Sampling
This section details the methodology and key insights gained from interviews with financial
advisors and retail investors. It outlines the diverse backgrounds of the interviewees and
how their experiences and priorities informed the structure and content of our comprehen-
sive Financial Context Dataset. Our interviews with financial advisors and retail investors
provided valuable insights that were instrumental in constructing a comprehensive dataset
of financial queries. The advisors we spoke with represented a diverse professional land-
scape, including those working for the wealth management divisions of banks serving their
established customer base, and independent private advisors catering to individual clients.
There were 4 advisors working for banks and 6 were independent advisors. Interestingly,
most of the independent advisors had transitioned to their current practice from a profes-
sional setup, where they gained experience as wealth managers typically for more than 5
years. The educational backgrounds of all advisors were robust, encompassing Masters in
Business Administration (MBA), Masters in Finance, Chartered Financial Analysts (CFA)
and Chartered Accountancy (CA), with some even holding engineering and science degrees,
highlighting a strong analytical foundation. All the advisors interviewed managed more
than 100 relationships. All of the advisors that we interviewed were registered with the
regulator (Securities and Exchange Board of India) after passing exams specified by the reg-
ulatory authority. The eligibility requires individuals to have Bachelor’s/Master’s degrees
or professional qualifications. The role of advisors, while overlapping in certain functions,
differs significantly from that of another category of individuals-relationship managers-who
are often unregistered with the regulatory authority. These interviews shed light on sev-
eral critical aspects of a financial advisor’s practice, directly informing the types of queries
included in our dataset. A primary focus for advisors when engaging with new clients is
to first ascertain if the client has made investments in the past, to what extent they un-
derstand investments, what is their risk profile, and what are their goals. This is followed
by understanding their liquidity requirements, investment horizons, preferences for asset
classes and overall financial situation to tailor a suitable portfolio. The client base itself
is varied, ranging from “passive investors” who delegate most of the investment decisions
to advisors due to a lack of technical market knowledge or time, to “active investors” who
engage in frequent and in-depth technical discussions about company financials and play
a more hands-on role in decision-making. This distinction is crucial for understanding the
varying levels of detail and technicality required in responses to client queries. Further-
more, the advisors consistently emphasized the paramount importance of staying abreast
of the latest financial news. They highlighted that any significant event, whether micro
or macroeconomic, could have a direct and immediate impact on their clients’ portfolios,
necessitating constant monitoring, analysis and update. This underscored the need for the
dataset to include queries that require real-time financial data and event-driven information
to provide comprehensive and timely answers. The retail investors whom we interviewed
primarily represented three classes: 10% large investments (more than USD 250,000 invest-
ments), 20% medium investments (between USD 50,000 to 250,000 investments), 70% low
investments (less than USD 50,000 investments). All investments were in Indian Rupees
(INR) that we have converted approximately into US dollars (USD). There was variability

(2020)), CD (Mariko et al. (2020)), FNXL (Sharma et al. (2023)), and FSRL (Lamm
et al. (2018)) datasets, designed to assess the LLM’s capacity for extracting structured
information from unstructured text.
2. Textual Analysis: This section focuses on evaluating textual understanding and an-
alytical capabilities, utilizing the Financial Phrase Bank (FPB) (Malo et al. (2014)),
FiQA-SA (Maia et al. (2018)), TSA (Cortis et al. (2017)), Headlines (Sinha and

(2020)), and MLESG (Chen et al. (2023)) datasets.
3. Question Answering: This category assesses the LLM’s ability to comprehend and
