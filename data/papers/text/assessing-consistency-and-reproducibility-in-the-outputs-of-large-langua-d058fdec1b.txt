Abstract: This study provides the first comprehensive assessment of consistency and reproducibility in 
Large Language Model (LLM) outputs in finance and accounting tasks. We evaluate how consistently 
LLMs produce outputs given identical inputs through extensive experimentation with 50 independent runs 
across five common tasks: classification, sentiment analysis, summarization, text generation, and 
prediction. Using three OpenAI models (GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4o-mini, and GPT-4o), we generate over 3.4 
million outputs using diverse financial source texts and data, covering MD&As, FOMC statements, finance 
news articles, earnings call transcripts, and financial statements. Our findings reveal substantial but task -
dependent consistency, with binary classification and sentiment analysis achieving near -perfect 
reproducibility, while complex tasks show greater variability. More advanced models do not consistently 
demonstrate b etter consistency and reproducibility, with task -specific patterns emerging. LLMs 
significantly outperform expert human annotators in consistency and maintain high agreement even where 
human experts significantly disagree. We further find that simple aggregation strategies across 3 -5 runs 
dramatically improve consistency. We also find that aggregation may come with an additional benefit of 
improved accuracy for sentiment analysis when using newer models. Simulation analysis reveals that 
despite measurable inconsistency  in LLM outputs, downstream statistical inferences remain remarkably 
robust. These findings address concerns about what we term “G-hacking,” the selective reporting of 
favorable outcomes from multiple generative AI runs, by demonstrating that such risks are relatively low 
for finance and accounting tasks. 
 
Keywords: Generative AI (GenAI), Large Language Models (LLMs), ChatGPT, Reproducibility,

1. Introduction 
The integration of artificial intelligence, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), into finance and 
accounting research has surged dramatically in recent years. These sophisticated models, exemplified by 
OpenAI's GPT series, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in analyzing financial text and generating 
insights from vast amounts of unstructured data (Dong et al. 2024). From classifying financial text (Hansen 
and Kazinnik 2024), analyzing sentiment (Lopez-Lira and Tang 2024), summarizing corporate disclosures 
(Kim et al. 2024a), to predicting future earnings or stock returns  (E. X. Li et al. 2024) , LLMs promise to 
revolutionize how researchers approach financial text analysis (de Kok 2025) and how investors process 
information and make investment decisions (Blankespoor et al. 2024). 
However, as with any emerging methodological innovation, questions of consistency and 
reproducibility remain paramount concerns for the research community. The foundation of scientific 
inquiry rests on the principle that research findings should be reproducible; yet, this fundamental tenet faces 
a unique challenge when applied to research utilizing LLMs. Unlike deterministic algorithms with fixed 
outputs for given inputs, LLMs generate outputs through probabilistic token selection processes, meaning 
that even identical inputs with identical prompts may yield different results across different runs. This 
inherent stochasticity raises questions about the validity and reproducibility of research findings that rely 
on LLM-generated outputs. 
While a growing body of literature has explored LLMs' performance in finance and accounting 
applications, surprisingly little attention has been paid to this critical issue of reproducibility. Existing 
studies have primarily focused on comparing LLMs' perf ormance to traditional methods, evaluating their 
capabilities in specific tasks, or addressing potential biases in their outputs (Levy 2024, Sarkar and Vafa 
2024, Bini et al. 2025) . The broader finance and accounting literature has grappled with reproducibility 
challenges long before the advent of LLMs (Harvey 2019, Menkveld et al. 2024, Gow 2025). However, the 
fundamental question of whether LLM -based analyses can produce consistent, reproducible results , a 
prerequisite for their integration into rigorous academic research, remains largely unexplored. 
While our study focuses specifically on LLM consistency and reproducibility, it is worth noting that 
the broader machine learning literature in finance has developed various approaches to address 
interpretability and validation challenges. Deep learning te chniques have shown success in asset pricing 
(Chen et al. 2024), mortgage risk prediction (Sadhwani et al. 2021), mutual fund performance evaluation 
(Kaniel et al. 2023), credit rating transitions (Kumar et al. 2025), among many other applications. Giesecke 
et al. (2025) address interpretability concerns across these applications by developing model -agnostic 
significance tests for feature importance. Cong and Yang (2024) combine human perception measures with

language embeddings to analyze discrimination patterns. These important methodological advances provide 
valuable frameworks for understanding machine learning models in finance. LLMs, with their probabilistic 
text generation processes, introduce additional  considerations for reproducibility that complement these 
existing validation approaches, partly motivating our systematic investigation of output consistency across 
multiple runs. 
The reproducibility concerns extend beyond academic settings to practical applications in investment 
and financial decision -making contexts. Blankespoor et al. (2024) document the widespread adoption of 
Generative AI among retail investors, with nearly half of surveyed investors already using these 
technologies for tasks ranging from interpreting financial information to calculating ratios and assessing 
sentiment. For these users, inconsistent outputs coul d lead to conflicting interpretations of the same 
underlying information, potentially leading to mis-informed investment decisions. 
Our study addresses this critical gap by conducting the first comprehensive assessment of the 
consistency and reproducibility of LLM outputs in common finance and accounting tasks. We choose three 
GPT models from OpenAI , namely, GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4o-mini, and GPT-4o, due to the current 
dominance of GPT models in both research (Dong et al. 2024)  and among investors (Blankespoor et al. 
2024). Our assessment covers five key tasks: classification, sentiment analysis, summarization, text 
generation, and prediction. We employ a rigorous experimental design involving 50 independent runs for 
each task conducted over 50 days, using different random seeds to capture the models' inherent 
stochasticity. The analysis covers diverse financial text sources, including MD&As, FOMC statements, 
financial news, earnings conference call transcripts, and financial statements. We generate more than 3.4 
million outputs through approximately 750,000 API calls, creating one of the most comprehensive 
assessments of LLM reproducibility to d ate, based on more than 80 million pairwise comparisons across 
different runs.  
For each task, we implement a zero-shot prompting approach, focusing solely on the models' inherent 
capabilities without task -specific examples or fine -tuning. This methodological choice provides a more 
stringent test of the models' fundamental understandi ng and generalization capabilities while minimizing 
potential biases from few-shot examples or Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. To assess consistency, we 
employ a comprehensive set of metrics tailored to each task's output type, including inter -rater agreement 
measures (Fleiss’ Kappa, Krippendorff's Alpha), correlation metrics ( intra-class, Pearson, Spearman, and 
Concordance), variation metrics ( mean relative differences), and other run -level and document -level 
agreement metrics, for comprehensive analysis of both categorical and continuous outputs. We also assess 
the consistency of text outputs in semantic similarity and in characteristics such as tone and length. We 
measure semantic similarity using cosine similarities calculated from text embeddings generated by a state-

of-the-art embedding model (jina-embeddings-v3), and we measure tone using both traditional word lists  
(Loughran and McDonald 2011) and FinBERT (Huang et al. 2023). 
Our findings reveal substantial , but task-dependent, consistency across models and tasks. For binary 
classification tasks like identifying forward -looking statements, both GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4o-mini 
achieve near-perfect consistency (Fleiss’ Kappa > 0.93), with identical classifications across all 50 runs for 
over 92% of sentences. Sentiment analysis similarly demonstrates high consistency (Fleiss’ Kappa > 0.94) 
across all text types, although with notable differences in sentiment distribution between models. More 
complex tasks show greater variability, wit h multi-class classification of FOMC statements exhibiting 
moderate consistency (Fleiss’ Kappa = 0.86-0.91), while numerical predictions of future earnings display 
substantial variation in point estimates from less advanced models. 
Importantly, we find that more advanced models do not consistently outperform their predecessors in 
consistency. While GPT-4o demonstrates superior consistency in numerical predictions relative to earlier 
models, GPT-3.5-turbo unexpectedly outperforms GPT-4o-mini in multi-class classification of FOMC 
statements. These findings suggest that model sophistication does not necessarily translate to increased 
consistency, and task-specific considerations should guide model selection. 
For summarization and text generation, we observe high semantic consistency (mean cosine similarity 
> 0.94) but moderate variation in length and tone. The semantic content of summaries and generated 
answers remains highly stable across runs, but word count s can vary by 8 -15% on average, with 
substantially higher variation for some documents. These results suggest that while LLMs maintain 
consistent core messaging, researchers should exercise caution when analyzing structural or stylistic 
features of generated texts. 
We conduct further analyses to address three additional important research questions. First, we 
investigate whether LLMs exhibit similar inconsistency patterns to expert human annotators in 
classification tasks. Using the Financial PhraseBank dataset  (Malo et al. 2013, Yoo 2024) , we compare 
consistency in sentiment classification between LLMs and human experts. We find that LLMs demonstrate 
higher consistency than human experts in over 50% of cases, with human experts outperforming LLMs in 
fewer than 9% of cases. Most strikingly, LLMs maintain high agreement levels (96.8-98.3%) even for cases 
where human expert annotators significantly disagree. This suggests that LLMs develop more deterministic 
classification boundaries that remain stable regardless of the inherent ambiguity in texts that causes human 
disagreement, making them potentially valuable tools for tasks requiring consistent classification. 
Second, we examine whether aggregation strategies across multiple runs can enhance consistency. For 
both categorical and continuous outputs, we find that even a modest level of aggregation substantially

improves consistency.1 The most dramatic improvements occur at an aggregation level of three to five runs, 
with majority class strength (i.e., consensus) for classification tasks increasing from approximately 86% to 
94% for GPT-3.5-turbo and from 82% to 91% for GPT-4o-mini. For continuous variables like text length, 
the variation between model runs  decreases dramatically from approximately 8.5% with a single run to 
about 3% with just five aggregated runs . We also find that aggregation can improve the accuracy of 
sentiment analysis when using newer models. These findings provide practical guidance for users seeking 
to balance computational costs with consistency requirements, as most consistency benefits can be captured 
with minimal additional computational overhead , possibly even with the added benefit of improved 
accuracy. 
Finally, we evaluate how LLM output variation affects downstream statistical analyses. Through 
extensive simulation with 10,000 iterations for totally 10 million regressions, we examine how variability 
in LLM-generated document lengths propagates into regression results. Despite measurable inconsistency 
in summary lengths, we find that regression coefficients appear to be unbiased, t-statistics maintain their 
expected distributions, and inference validity is preserved at remarkably high rates: 97.35% of all inferences 
drawn about the significance of length effects are correct, with Type I and Type II errors occurring at only 
1.28% and 1.38% of the time, respectively. Most notably, when significant effects are detected, they almost 
never show an incorrect sign direction. These results provide strong reassurance that LLM -based textual 
analysis can be reliably incorporated into quantitative research frameworks, even when analyzing subtle 
effects where measurement precision is crucial. 
This paper makes several important contributions to the emerging literature on LLMs in finance and 
accounting research (e.g., Cong and Zhu 2024, Jha et al. 2024, 2025, Sheng et al. 2025) . First, it provides 
robust empirical evidence on the degree of consistency in LLM outputs for common research tasks, offering 
researchers essential guidance on the reliability of these models when incorporated into their 
methodological toolkits. Our findings establish benchmark expectations for reproducibility across different 
tasks, allowing researchers to anticipate potential variability and implement appropriate validation 
procedures. 
Second, we provide comprehensive benchmarking of LLM consistency across multiple model versions 
(GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT -4o-mini, and GPT -4o) and common research tasks. Our analysis reveals that 
advanced models do not necessarily demonstrate improved reproducibility al ongside their enhanced 
capabilities, offering users essential guidance for model selection that considers both performance and 
output stability.

Third, our additional analyses offer import ant insights into the incorporation of LLMs in to data 
processing pipelines. We document that LLMs exhibit consistency patterns significantly different from 
human experts, shedding light on their distinct role in financial text annotation. We demonstrate that simple 
aggregation strategies effectively mitigate inconsistency , providing users with practical implementation

Taken together, these contributions have significant implications for LLM deployment in finance and 
accounting research. They provide researchers with both validation of LLM reliability and actionable 
strategies for implementation, addressing key methodological concerns in this emerging field. Our findings 
also address emerging concerns about what we term “G-hacking” in LLM-based research, analogous to p-
hacking in statistical analyses. We define G -hacking as occurring when researchers generate multiple 
outputs from generative LLMs for the same task and cherry-pick results that support their hypotheses while 
discarding contradictory outcomes. Our analysis suggests that the risk of G -hacking is relatively low for 
most finance and accounting applications.

2. Background, Related Work, and Research Questions 
The application of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in finance and accounting research 
has gained significant traction in recent years (Bochkay et al. 2022) . However, the use of LLMs in these 
fields is still at an early stage (Dong et al. 2024) , particularly regarding issues of consistency  and 
reproducibility. This section reviews relevant literature to contextualize our study. 
2.1 LLMs in Finance and Accounting 
The emergence of sophisticated LLMs has revolutionized how researchers process and analyze financial 
texts, offering unprecedented capabilities for tasks ranging from classification to prediction (Dong et al. 
2024, de Kok 2025). For example, Hansen and Kazinnik (2024) demonstrate LLMs' superior performance 
in analyzing Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) statements. Multiple studies have  established 
LLMs' ability to extract sentiment from financial texts with greater nuance than dictionary-based and other 
ML-based approaches (e.g., Bond et al. 2025, J. Chen et al. 2023, Lopez-Lira and Tang 2024).


[Page 7]
6 
 
Recent studies demonstrate LLMs' diverse applications in finance and accounting research. These 
include classifying ESG disclosures and tax discussions (Li et al. 2023, Choi and Kim 2023) , extracting 
data from unstructured text (H. Li et al. 2024, Wang and Wang 2024, Levich and Knust 2025), summarizing 
MD&As and assessing risk from earnings calls (Kim et al. 2024a, b), forecasting financial performance (E. 
X. Li et al. 2024), estimating core earnings (Shaffer and Wang 2024), analyzing employee reviews (Brown 
et al. 2025), and extracting firm investment and macroeconomic indicators (Jha et al. 2024, 2025) . These 
findings demonstrate generative AI's growing relevance to finance and accounting research  and decision-
making.  
2.2 Reproducibility Concerns in Finance and Accounting Research 
Reproducibility and replicability  have been a longstanding concern in finance and accounting research, 
long before the introduction of LLMs. Harvey (2019) discusses p-hacking and replicability issues in finance 
research. Menkveld et al. (2024) introduce the concept of “nonstandard errors ” to characterize the 
variability in research outcomes  when different research teams test the same hypotheses . Using this 
framework, Pérignon et al. (2024) find only 52% of finance results could be precisely reproduced even with 
provided code and documentation. 
Reproducibility success varies substantially across finance subfields. While Jensen et al. (2023) offer 
optimistic findings in asset pricing and Chen and Zimmermann (2022) present positive assessments, Hou, 
Xue, and Zhang (2020) and Linnainmaa and Roberts (2018) find numerous documented effects fail under 
stringent statistical tests or out-of-sample testing. 
In accounting, Hail et al. (2020) survey 136 researchers, finding 52% considered irreproducibility a 
major problem, with respondents estimating only 46% of published results were replicable and 59% 
reproducible. Gow (2025) documents p-hacking prevalence in accounting research, suggesting replication 
could significantly improve empirical work. Cross -disciplinarily, Fišar et al. (2024) find reproducibility 
rates improved after a premium business journal implemented data and code policies, although data 
accessibility and documentation remain barriers. 
These reproducibility challenges in finance and accounting take on heightened significance with the 
integration of LLMs into research workflows. LLMs introduce additional layers of complexity through their 
stochastic generation processes, potentially exacerbating existing concerns. The stochastic nature of LLMs 
creates particular vulnerabilities to G -hacking, as the inherent randomness in model outputs provides 
researchers with plausible deniability for selective reporting. Unlike traditional p -hacking, whe re 
manipulation often leaves statistical traces, the opacity of LLM generation processes could more easily 
mask selective reporting practices. This emerging concern underscores the importance of establishing 
baseline expectations for LLM output consistency across different tasks and model types.

2.3 LLM Consistency and Reproducibility 
Recent studies have explored various aspects of LLMs and their applications in finance, accounting, and 
many other disciplines. However, the consistency and reproducibility of LLMs' performance has not been 
extensively investigated, particularly in the context of finance and accounting research or applications.2  
Aguda et al. (2024) investigate the potential of LLMs as data annotators for extracting relations in 
financial documents. They emphasize the importance of customizing prompts for each relation group and 
introduce a reliability index to identify outputs that may require exper t attention. This work provides 
valuable insights into using LLMs for structured information extraction but does not examine output 
consistency across multiple runs. Reiss (2023) examines the consistency of ChatGPT's zero -shot 
capabilities for text annotation and classification, finding that even minor wording alterations in prompts 
or repeating identical inputs can lead to varying outputs. The study underscore s the need for thorough 
validation against human -annotated data and represent s one of the few works directly addressing LLM 
consistency, although with a narrower scope than our comprehensive assessment. 
Gilardi et al. (2023) and Törnberg (2023) demonstrate that ChatGPT outperforms crowd-workers and 
experts in annotating political Twitter messages with zero -shot learning, achieving higher accuracy, 
reliability, and equal or lower bias. Exploring another dimension of LLM applications, Rossi et al. (2024) 
investigate the use of LLM-generated synthetic data in social science and design research. Their work raises 
important questions about the validity of LLM -generated content for research purposes but does not 
systematically evaluate output consistency. 
Addressing a critical concern in financial applications, Sarkar and Vafa (2024) discuss the issue of 
temporal lookahead bias in empirical analysis that uses outputs from pretrained language models. Yoo 
(2024) evaluates the reliability of self -reported confidence scores from LLMs in sentiment analysis of 
finance news. This work highlight s important limitations in LLMs' self -assessment capabilities but does 
not address the fundamental question of output reproducibility. Adding to these insights, Levy (2024) aims 
to provide rationale for the documented superior performance of generative AI systems in accounting and 
finance-related tasks, highlighting the poor numerical reasoning of LLMs and the presence of look -ahead 
bias in prediction tasks. Bini et al. (2025) find that LLMs exhibit systematic behavioral biases in economic 
decisions and propose ways to mitigate such biases.  
While these studies have provided valuable insights into the capabilities and limitations of LLMs, they 
have primarily focused on aspects such as performance, overconfidence, look -ahead bias, numerical 
reasoning abilities, and behavioral bias. In contrast, our study aims to fill a critical gap in the literature by 
specifically assessing the consistency and reproducibility of LLMs' performance across multiple runs for

2.4 Practical Implications for Financial Decision-Making 
The reproducibility concern extends beyond academia to practical investment and financial decision -
making. Blankespoor et al. (2024) find nearly half of retail investors already use generative AI for 
explaining financial information (44.2%), defining terms (41.2%), searching company mentions (28.7%), 
and retrieving data (28.2%). Advanced applications include calculating ratios (21.4%), summarizing 
documents (21.0%), identifying trends (16.4%), and sentiment analysis (14.9%), with 27.4% seeking 
investment recommendations.  
The reproducibility challenges of LLMs are particularly concerning for these specific applications. For 
investors relying on LLMs to explain financial concepts, identify trends, calculate ratios, or assess 
sentiment, inconsistent outputs could lead to conflicting interpretations of the same underlying information. 
This variability creates potential risks for decision -makers who rely on these tools for time -sensitive or 
high-stakes financial decisions, especially the more than one-quarter of users who directly seek investment 
advice from these systems. 
2.5 Research Questions 
Given the critical importance of this issue and the absence of systematic evidence, this study investigates 
the consistency and reproducibility of LLMs for common finance and accounting tasks. We systematically 
assess output variability across multiple runs and compare performance across different model versions. 
The following five research questions guide our investigation: 
RQ1: How consistent are LLM outputs for common finance and accounting tasks across multiple runs? 
This question evaluates the fundamental consistency of LLMs when provided with identical inputs and 
prompts across multiple executions. By subjecting the models to repeated runs on the same tasks, we aim 
to quantify output variability and assess their ability to produce consistent, reproducible results. This 
analysis is essential for establishing baseline expectations regarding LLM robustness in finance and 
accounting applications, where consistency is a prerequisite for scientific validity  and reliable decision -
making. 
RQ2: How do different versions of LLMs compare in terms of output consistency? 
This question examines whether more advanced model versions demonstrate improved consistency 
alongside their enhanced capabilities. By comparing output reliability across different models, we test 
whether consistency improves with model sophistication or follows different patterns. These insights are 
critical for researchers and practitioners weighing trade -offs between cost, performance, and consistency 
when selecting appropriate models for specific applications.


[Page 10]
9 
 
RQ3: Do LLMs exhibit consistency patterns similar to human annotators? 
This question investigates whether LLMs mimic human annotation patterns, particularly in contexts 
where human annotators demonstrate significant disagreements. By analyzing model outputs on datasets 
with documented human inconsistencies, we assess whether LLMs struggle with the same ambiguous cases 
or manifest distinct variability patterns. These findings have important implications for determining the role 
of LLMs in financial text analysis. 
RQ4: Can aggregation strategies across multiple runs enhance LLM output consistency? 
This question explores practical mitigation strategies for LLM inconsistency through output 
aggregation. By implementing and evaluating various aggregation techniques , including majority voting 
for categorical outputs and averaging for continuous predictions, we assess their effectiveness in enhancing 
result consistency. Additionally, we examine the effect of aggregation on accuracy. These findings provide 
actionable guidance for researchers and practitioners seeking to increase consistency in LLM -based 
analyses while balancing computational costs. 
RQ5: How does LLM output variation affect downstream statistical analyses? 
This question examines both the propagation of LLM output variation  into subsequent analytical 
workflows and addresses concerns about selective reporting of favorable LLM outputs . We assess how 
variability in model outputs influences statistical significance, effect sizes, and substantive conclusions 
when these outputs serve as variables in regression analyses . This quantifies the potential implications of 
incorporating LLM-generated variables into broader research frameworks. This analysis also allows us to 
evaluate the severity of G-hacking concerns in finance and accounting applications. 
3. Experimental Design 
In this section, we describe our experimental design, covering task selection, model choice, prompting 
strategies, and evaluation metrics. Specific design choices for each task, such as sample selection, prompts,

3.1 Task Selection 
Our task selection is guided by a comprehensive literature review conducted by Dong et al. (2024), who 
find that numerous studies have utilized LLMs to carry out tasks such as classification, sentiment analysis, 
summarization, text generation, and prediction. We choose these tasks for our study. To assess the 
consistency and reproducibility of these tasks, we use a variety of text commonly employed in finance and 
accounting research, including FOMC statements, financial news articles, earnings conference call  
transcripts, and MD&As. For the prediction task, we supply LLMs with condensed comparative financial 
statements in addition to MD&A summaries.

3.1.1 Classification and Sentiment Analysis 
Classification involves categorizing data into predefined classes. Recent studies demonstrate LLMs' 
effectiveness in classifying financial texts, e.g., identifying ESG disclosures (Li et al. 2023) , classifying 
management presentations as facts/opinions (Kuroki et al. 2023), determining earnings call discussion types 
(Jia et al. 2024), assessing firm characteristics (Bernard et al. 2023, Dasgupta et al. 2023) , identifying tax 
audit disclosures (Choi and Kim 2023), and classifying mutual fund manager statements (Wang 2023). 
For binary classification, we examine forward -looking statements in MD&As, which are useful for 
predicting future earnings and cash flows. Prior research used dictionary -based or machine learning 
approaches (e.g., Li 2010, Muslu et al. 2014, Bozanic et al. 2018, Brown et al. 2024) . For multi -class 
classification, we analyze FOMC statements, where Hansen and Kazinnik (2024) have demonstrated LLMs' 
superiority in this task. 
Fundamentally a classification task, sentiment analysis classifies emotional tone as positive, negative, 
or neutral using dictionary -based methods (Loughran and McDonald 2011)  or ML approaches like 
FinBERT (Huang et al. 2023) . Numerous studies use LLMs to extract sentiment from various financial 
texts (Breitung et al. 2023, Alonso -Robisco and Carbó  2023, Smales 2023, Bae et al. 2024) , and apply 
LLM-enabled sentiment analysis to forecast stock trends and evaluate trading strategies (Álvarez-Dí ez et 
al. 2024, Bond et al. 2025, J. Chen et al. 2023, B. Chen et al. 2023, Kirtac and Germano 2024, Lopez-Lira 
and Tang 2024). LLMs consistently outperform traditional methods (Bond et al. 2025, J. Chen et al. 2023, 
Hu et al. 2023, Kirtac and Germano 2024, Lopez -Lira and Tang 2024) , although studies note potential 
biases requiring careful implementation (Leippold 2023, Hu et al. 2023, Glasserman and Lin 2023).  
For this common task , we use multiple text sources, as prior studies have done. The first source is 
sentences from the Financial PhraseBank (Malo et al. 2013, Yoo 2024), which consists of sentences from 
financial news articles . The benefit of this dataset is that it not only has human labels but also provides 
information about disagreements among expert human raters. This information allows us to additionally

3.1.2 Summarization and Text Generation 
Summarization involves generating a concise summary of a longer text while preserving its key 
information. Most LLMs can now create reliable summaries that capture the depth of the original text. Kim, 
Muhn, and Nikolaev (Kim et al. 2024a, b) use GPT-3.5-turbo to summarize MD&As and conference call 
transcripts. For this task, we ask the models to summarize MD&As and earnings call presentations. 
Text generation involves creating new text based on a given prompt or context, often aiming to mimic 
human-like writing or response. Bai et al. (2023) use LLMs to answer questions raised by equity analysts 
during earnings call Q&A sessions, while Wu, Dong, Li, and Shi (2023) employ ChatGPT to generate 
analyses of textual loan assessments. For this task, we ask the models, serving as executives of the company 
to answer questions posed by financial analysts at earnings conferences. 
3.1.3 Prediction 
Prediction involves forecasting future events or outcomes, such as stock price movements or a 
company's future earnings, based on historical data and patterns. Li, Tu, and Zhou (2023) find that earnings 
forecasts generated by GPT-4 exhibit greater forecast errors than analyst consensus, but its performance 
improves for firms with better information environments or higher -quality disclosures. Comlekci et al.  
(2023) use ChatGPT to forecast future financial performance and dividends of public companies.  For this 
task, we ask the models to predict future earnings.  
3.2 Model Choice 
In this study, we focus on GPT models provided by OpenAI, specifically GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4o-mini, 
and GPT-4o. This choice is informed by Dong et al.'s (2024) finding that most existing studies on LLMs in 
accounting and finance use these models, as well as OpenAI's dominant market share among retail investors 
(Blankespoor et al. 2024). We also consider cost-effectiveness in our model selection. GPT-3.5 and GPT-
4o-mini are relatively less expensive, while GPT-4o is more capable but also significantly more costly

the consistency across models and test whether newer or more advanced models exhibit greater consistency.

3.2.1 Prompting Strategies and Parameter Setting 
This study employs zero-shot prompting across all tasks to evaluate the inherent capabilities of LLMs 
without task -specific examples or fine -tuning. This methodological choice is motivated by several 
considerations. First, zero -shot prompting provides a mo re stringent test of the model's fundamental 
understanding and generalization capabilities, as it requires the model to perform tasks solely based on 
instructions without exemplars. Second, this approach minimizes potential biases that might be introduced 
through few-shot examples or Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting , which could inadvertently guide the 
model toward specific patterns or responses. Third, zero -shot prompting offers greater operational 
consistency across different tasks, facilitating more direct comparisons of model performance across 
diverse applications. For parameters, we set the temperature to zero for maximum reproducibility, a choice 
commonly made by most researchers, while keeping other parameters at their defaults. 
3.2.2 Experimental Runs and Experiment Period 
We choose to conduct 50 runs for each task to balance cost, statistical power, and the inherent 
randomness of LLMs. This choice aligns with our sample size selection for each run, detailed in the task -
specific design provided in Online Appendix A. By incorporating large sample sizes across numerous runs, 
we capture both within-run and across-run variation, strengthening the generalizability of our findings. 
These experiments span 50 days between December 27, 2024, and February 15, 2025, conducted at 
varying times to ensure diverse conditions. Each run utilize s a different seed to maintain independence 
between runs. We intentionally avoid using identical seeds across runs, as our goal is not to assess 
reproducibility given the same seed, but rather to evaluate consistency under varying conditions and the 
inherent randomness fundamental to generative AI models. 
3.3 Evaluation Metrics 
We evaluate consistency using a comprehensive set of metrics tailored to each task's output type: categorical 
variables, continuous variables, or texts. For text  outputs, we examine the consistency of characteristics 
such as similarity, length, and tone, where similarity and length are continuous variables and tone is 
categorical. 
As shown in Panel A of Table 3, we use  a wide variety of  metrics covering overall inter -rater 
agreements, run -level agreements, and document -level agreements. The overall inter -rater agreement 
metrics provide an overall summary metric of consistency, and they include Fleiss’ Kappa (Fleiss 1971) 
and Krippendorff's Alpha  (Krippendorff 2011). These metrics quantify the overall agreement across all 
runs, with higher values indicating stronger agreement.


[Page 14]
13 
 
The run-level agreements provide summary metrics for pairwise consistency between runs, including 
Cohen's Kappa scores and agreement percentages. Cohen's Kappa adjusts for chance agreement, making it 
more stringent than simple percentage agreement, with values from -1 to 1 where higher indicates stronger 
agreement. The run -pair agreement percentage shows the proportion of documents classified identically 
between runs, offering an intuitive consistency measure.  
At the document level, we measure percentage of perfect agreement, document -wise agreement, 
majority class strength, and classification uncertainty. These metrics focus on individual documents rather 
than run-pairs, showing how consistently each document i s classified across all runs. Perfect agreement 
identifies documents with complete consensus across 50 runs, while document -wise agreement measures 
the percentage of run -pairs yielding identical classifications for a document. Majority class strength 
quantifies the dominance of the most frequent classification, and classification uncertainty uses entropy to 
measure variability where disagreement exists.  
Appendix A illustrates calculation methods for both run -level and document -level metrics. From 50 
runs yielding 1,225 possible run-pairs, we report aggregated statistics (mean, median, standard deviation) 
to characterize the distribution. For document-level metrics, each document has an agreement measure, and 
we similarly provide summary statistics (mean, median, standard deviation) across all documents in each 
task. See Appendix B for detailed metric definitions. 
For continuous outputs (Panel B, Table 3), we use metrics designed to assess numerical consistency 
across runs, organized into four categories: overall inter -run reliability, correlation metrics, run -level 
variation, and document -level variation. The Intra class Correlation Coefficient (ICC2, type 2) measures 
overall inter-run reliability, assessing absolute agreement rather than just relative consistency, with values 
near one indicating strong reliability. ICC2 accounts for both systematic and random differ ences between 
runs, making it suitable for determining if runs can be used interchangeably. We also employ Concordance, 
Pearson, and Spearman correlations.  
We assess run -level variation using run -pair MARD (Mean Absolute Relative Difference), which 
measures the typical magnitude of differences between run pairs as a proportion of their average value, with 
lower percentages indicating smaller relative variatio ns. At the document level, we measure documents 
with identical output (%) and document-wise MARD (%). The identical output percentage quantifies how 
often runs produce exactly the same numerical value for a document, while document -wise MARD 
calculates the average relative difference across all run pairs for each document. We provide descriptive 
statistics for both run-level and document-level metrics across all run pairs and documents.  
For summarization and text generation tasks, the model outputs are texts. To evaluate the consistency 
of these outputs, we examine several common textual attributes , including length (word counts), tone 
(positive, negative, or neutral), and semantic similarity (cosine similarity), as shown in Panel C of Table 3.


[Page 15]
14 
 
Since length and semantic similarity are continuous variables, their results are evaluated using the metrics 
for continuous variables. To determine the tone of generated texts , we use both a lexicon-based approach 
(Loughran and McDonald 2011) and FinBERT (Huang et al. 2023). For each text, we classify its tone and 
evaluate the result using our chosen metrics for categorical variables. 
To evaluate semantic similarity, we utilize a state -of-the-art embedding model, jina-embeddings-v3, 
which offers a good balance between computational efficiency and performance (Sturua et al. 2024). The 
process involves converting each generated text into high-dimensional embeddings and computing pairwise 
cosine similarities between runs. This approach allows us to quantify how consistently the model maintains 
core semantic content across multiple generations. We assess the consistency of the similarity scores using 
our chosen metrics for continuous variables.

To address our second research question regarding inter-model consistency differences, we provide a more

universal trend. This has important implications for model selection in financial text analysis applications. 
For most classification tasks, GPT-4o-mini demonstrates superior consistency compared to GPT-3.5-

In this sub-section, we report the results for RQ3 regarding whether LLMs outperform expert human

human agreement levels. The dataset comes with four levels of agreements, namely, 50%, 66%, 75%, and 
100%, where 50% means 50% of human annotators agree on a label, e.g., a positive sentiment. We measure 
the level of agreement in the same manner whereby we calculate the agreement over the majority class for 
each document. 
As shown in Panel A of Figure 1 , GPT-3.5-turbo demonstrates greater consistency than human

with ties accounting for 42.7% of cases. These results indicate that both models, particularly GPT-4o-mini, 
achieve higher consistency than human annotators in most cases.

However, even for sentences with low human agreement (50-75%), model agreement remains remarkably

maintain consistent classifications even for sentences that human annotators find ambiguous or challenging

5.2 Aggregation Across Multiple Runs 
This sub-section reports results for RQ4 examining whether aggregating outputs from multiple runs of the 
same model increases consistency. We evaluate this in two contexts: classification tasks and summary 
length consistency.  
For classification tasks, we focus on multi -class classification of FOMC statements, as distinguishing 
among five distinct classes presents a more challenging scenario for evaluating model consistency. Our 
sample includes sentences where runs disagreed on labels. We randomly aggregate 2 -20 outputs from 50 
runs, using majority voting with tie -breaking via median rank (classes coded 0 -4 from 'Dovish' to 
'Hawkish'). This process generates 50 synthetic runs for calculating document-level metrics. 
Figure 2 shows clear improvements with aggregation. Panel A reveals that majority class strength 
increases substantially with minimal aggregation. The steepest gains occur with 3-5 runs, where GPT-3.5-
turbo improves from around 86% to 94% and GPT-4o-mini from 82% to 91%. At 20 aggregated runs, these 
reach 98% and 95.5%, respectively. Classification uncertainty (entropy) drops most sharply in the first few 
aggregated runs, with GPT-3.5-turbo maintaining lower uncertainty throughout. 
Panel B demonstrates enhanced consistency: perfect agreement jumps dramatically with just 5 
aggregated runs (GPT -3.5-turbo: 25% to 65%; GPT -4o-mini: 0% to 55%). At 20 runs, GPT -3.5-turbo 
achieves around 85% perfect agreement versus around 78% for GPT -4o-mini. Document-wise agreement 
scores similarly improve, exceeding 96% and 94% respectively at 20 runs. 
We apply similar aggregation methodology to MD&A summary length, averaging sampled runs rather 
than using majority voting for this continuous variable. Figure 3 demonstrates substantial benefits across 
all metrics. Correlation metrics (ICC2, CCC, Spearman, Pearson) show dramatic improvement within the 
first 5 aggregated runs. For example, CCC increases from 0.43 to 0.8, while others improve from 0.65-0.7 
to around 0.9. At 20 runs, all metrics converge between 0.95-0.98. Mean Run-pair MARD decreases from 
8.5% to 3% with 5 runs, reaching around 1.5% at 20 runs. 
These findings reveal that users do not need to implement extensive aggregation to enhance LLM 
consistency. Modest aggregation over 3-5 runs captures substantial benefit  in improved consistency, 
offering a valuable insight for balancing efficiency against reliability requirements. The results provide 
strong evidence that run aggregation effectively enhances LLM consistency across both classification and 
continuous output types, offering a practical guidance for researchers and practitioners.

5.4 Impact on Downstream Tasks 
Studies in finance and accounting increasingly incorporate LLM-generated variables in regression analyses. 
Our final research question (RQ5) examines how LLM output variation affects statistical analysis 
reliability. We implement a simulation -based methodology quantifying how length variability in LLM -
generated MD&A summaries propagates into regression analyses, evaluating impacts on coefficient 
estimates, statistical significance, and inference reliability. We scale summary length by MD&A length, 
generating an inverse bloatedness measure similar to Kim et al. (2024a). 
We employ Monte Carlo simulation with bootstrapping and leave-one-out validation to evaluate how 
LLM inconsistency affects downstream statistical analysis (Efron and Tibshirani 1994, Shao and Tu 2012, 
Hammersley 2013). Using the 50 runs from our consistency analyses, we generate 1,001 synthetic runs by 
randomly sampling summary lengths for each document. From these synthetic runs:  (1) we designate one 
run as "ground truth" to define the summary length variable; (2) we generate an outcome variable (y) with 
a known relationship to this length variable ; (3) we estimate the true coefficient by regressing “y” on the 
ground truth length variable; (4) We estimate 1,000 separate regressions for the remaining 1,000 synthetic 
runs and compare their length coefficients to the true coefficient . This entire process is repeated 10,000 
times, yielding 10 million total regressions that allow us to assess estimation accuracy and bias. 
Our simulation employs the following specifications designed to mirror challenging but realistic 
empirical finance and accounting research conditions. We set the length effect at 0.005, a deliberately small


[Page 21]
20 
 
effect size reflecting realistic detection challenges. The control variable (X) effect is set at 0.2 to ensure 
substantial control variable influence, with a confounding correlation of 0.5 that provides moderate 
correlation while avoiding multicollinearity . The signal -to-noise ratio is 0.5, meaning the error standard 
deviation is twice the combined signal strength. These parameters generate R -squared values of 
approximately 0.2, consistent with typical empirical studies in accounting and finance.  
Figure 4 presents our simulation results. Panel A demonstrates that coefficient estimates remain stable 
and unbiased despite length variability introduced by LLM processing. The estimated coefficient 
distribution closely aligns with true values, with points clustering tightly around the perfect agreement line. 
Panel B reveals similar stability for t-statistics, where the distributions of estimated and true values overlap 
substantially. The fitted trend line tracks the perfect agreement line almost exactly, indicating minimal bias 
in significance testing. 
Panel C shows that statistical inference remains highly reliable. The significance of length effects is 
correctly identified in 97.35% of cases, with Type I and Type II error rates of only 1.28% and 1.38%, 
respectively. The reliability heatmap indicates 86.15% correct sign identification for non -significant 
effects, while significant effects maintain proper direction virtually always (0% incorrect signs). Overall 
correct coefficient sign identification reaches 92.40%.6  
These findings provide important reassurance for researchers. While LLM summarization introduces 
measurable length inconsistency, the downstream statistical impact remains negligible. Regression 
coefficients appear unbiased, t -statistics maintain their expected distributions, and inference validity is 
preserved at high rates. This suggests that LLM-based document processing can be safely incorporated into 
quantitative research pipelines, even when analyzing subtle effects that require substantial measuremen t 
precision. 
6. Discussion and Conclusion 
This study provides the first comprehensive assessment of the consistency and reproducibility of  
outputs from commonly used LLMs  across common finance and accounting research tasks. Through 
extensive experimentation involving 50 independent runs for each task and generating over 3.4 million 
outputs, we have established several key findings regarding LLM reliability in financial text analysis. 
Our results demonstrate that LLM output consistency varies substantially across tasks. Binary 
classification and sentiment analysis achieve near-perfect reproducibility, while more complex tasks exhibit 
greater variability . Contrary to expectations, more advanced models do not consistently demonstrate 
superior reproducibility. For textual outputs, we observe high semantic consistency (mean cosine similarity

> 0.94) alongside moderate variation in structural features such as length and tone.  These findings have 
significant methodological implications. Researchers should consider task complexity when applying these 
models, and conduct task -specific evaluations of both performance and consistency when selecting 
appropriate models.  
Our additional analysis shows that LLMs outperform human annotators in consistency  in sentiment 
analysis. LLMs maintain high agreement levels (96.8-98.3%) even for sentences where human annotators 
significantly disagree. Additionally, aggregation strategies like majority voting for categorical outputs and 
averaging for continuous predictions can substantially improve consistency, with the most dramatic gains 
occurring within aggregation levels of 3-5 runs. Our simulation of downstream regression analyses shows 
that despite output variability, statistical inference remains highly reliable (97.35% correct inferences) with 
minimal Type I and Type II errors (approximately 1.3% each).  
Our findings have important implications for addressing what we term “G-hacking,” i.e., the selective 
reporting of favorable outcomes from multiple runs of a generative LLM. The high consistency we observe 
across most tasks suggests that G -hacking risks are relatively low for most finance and accounting 
applications, particularly for classification and sentiment analysis. 
While comprehensive, our study has limitations that suggest future research directions. We focus 
exclusively on OpenAI models using zero-shot prompting. Future studies can extend our analysis to other 
LLMs and prompting strategies. Additional finance and accounting applications beyond our five chosen 
tasks, such as stock return prediction , risk assessment, and fraud detection, also warrant investigation. 
Furthermore, examining how consistency patterns evolve as models are updated would provide insights 
into the trajectory of reliability improvements in LLM technology.  We also call for further research into 
whether aggregation over multiple runs can increase accuracy in addition to consistency.

Agreement Metric Calculation Illustration 
This appendix illustrates the calculation of two key agreement metrics used in our analysis: run -pair agreement and 
document-wise agreement. Both metrics are derived from pairwise comparisons but differ in their unit of analysis.  
Panel A: Run-pair agreement 
 
Run-pair agreement measures how consistently two runs classify the same documents. The figure shows Run 1 vs 
Run 2 across six documents. Both runs agree for Documents 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, while the two runs disagree for Document 
3.  
Since 5 documents match out of 6, the agreement rate = (5/6) ×  100 = 83%. This calculation is repeated for all 1,225 
possible run pairs. The mean run-pair agreement is the average of all these agreement rates, showing overall 
classification consistency.

Panel B: Document-wise agreement 
 
Document-wise agreement measures classification consistency for individual documents across multiple runs. For

agreement is the average of these scores across all documents.

Appendix B 
Metric Definitions 
Panel A: Sample size metrics 
Number of documents : The total number of documents used for a task. A document can be a sentence, paragraph, 
longer text (e.g., a full MD&A section), or a combination of multiple data types as in the case of Q&A answering and 
future earnings prediction. In our terminology, a  document represents a single unit of the input provided to an LLM, 
which generates an output based on this input according to the prompt.  
Number of runs : The number of independent attempts performed by an LLM on each document using different 
random seeds (50 in our experiments). 
Number of run pairs: The total number of unique pairs that can be formed from the runs, calculated as (n ×  (n-1))/2, 
where “n” is the number of runs. With 50 runs, this yields 1,225 pairs. Each pair represents a unique comparison 
between two different runs' outputs.  
Panel B: Categorical variables (class-level) 
Class distribution: The proportion of documents assigned to each class based on majority voting across runs.  
Class-specific agreements: For each class, the average agreement rate across documents that received that 
classification in any run. For example, the rate may be 98% for forward-looking and 95% for non-forward looking.  
Panel C: Categorical variables (inter-rater and run-level) 
Fleiss’ Kappa: A statistical measure assessing agreement between multiple runs while accounting for chance 
agreement. It ranges from -1 to 1, where 1 indicates perfect agreement and 0 indicates chance-level agreement. 
Krippendorff’s Alpha : A robust agreement measure for multiple runs that accounts for missing data and 
measurement level. Like Fleiss’s Kappa, it ranges from -1 to 1. 
Run-pair Cohen’s Kappa score: A pairwise Cohen's Kappa score between two runs, measuring overall consistency 
between them. This score ranges from -1 to 1, where higher values indicate greater consistency between runs. 
Mean run-pair Cohen’s Kappa score: The average of all 1,250 pairwise Cohen's Kappa scores calculated between 
all possible pairs of runs. This provides an overall measure of consistency across all runs.  
Run-pair agreement: The pairwise classification agreement rate between two runs, calculated as described in Panel 
A of Appendix A. This measures the proportion of documents that receive identical classifications when comparing 
two specific runs. 
Mean run-pair agreement: The average of all 1,250 pairwise classification agreement rates calculated between all 
possible pairs of 50 runs.  
Panel D: Categorical variables (document-level) 
Percentage of perfect agreement (%): Percentage of documents where all 50 runs assign the same class. 
Document-wise agreement (%): The percentage of run pairs (out of all 1,225 possible pairs among the 50 runs) that 
assign the same classification to a specific document. This measures the consistency of classification for individual 
documents across multiple runs. See Panel B of Appendix B for an illustration. 
Mean document -wise agreement (%): The average of all document -wise run agreement percentages across all 
documents in a sample. This aggregate metric quantifies how often, on average, any two randomly selected runs will 
agree on the classification of a document. 
Majority class strength (%): The percentage of runs that assign the most frequent classification to a document. For 
each document, we identify which classification appears most frequently across the 50 runs, then calculate what 
percentage of those runs assigned that majority classification. 
Mean majority class strength  (%): The average of majority class strength percentages across all documents in a 
sample. 
Classification uncertainty : The entropy of the classification distribution across runs for documents with 
disagreement, calculated using Shannon entropy. Higher values indicate greater classification variability across runs,


[Page 30]
29 
 
with maximum entropy when classifications are evenly distributed across all classes. Ranges from 0 (unanimous 
classification) to log₂(n) (even distribution across “n” classes). 
Mean classification uncertainty : The average of classification uncertainty (entropy) values across all documents 
which have any disagreement over the 50 runs. 
Panel E: Continuous variables (run-level) 
ICC2: Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (type 2), which measures the degree of consistency between ratings from 
different runs across all documents. Values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater consistency.  
Concordance correlation: The pairwise Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) between two runs. Values range 
from -1 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect agreement, 0 indicating no correlation, and -1 indicating perfect negative 
agreement.  
Mean concordance correlation: The average of all 1,225 pairwise Concordance Correlation Coefficients (CCC) 
calculated between all possible pairs among the 50 runs. 
Spearman correlation: The Spearman rank correlation coefficient between two runs. Values range from -1 to 1, with 
higher values indicating stronger rank-order consistency. 
Median Spearman correlation: The average of all 1,225 pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients calculated 
between all possible pairs among the 50 runs. 
Run-pair MARD (%): The Mean Absolute Relative Differences (MARD) between two runs, expressed as a 
percentage. For each document, the relative difference is calculated as: |A -B|/((|A|+|B|)/2) *100, where A and B are 
the values from the two runs and |x| indicates absolute value. These differences are then averaged across all documents 
in a sample.  
Mean run-pair MARD (%): The average run -pair MARDs between all 1,225 run -pair MARD values calculated 
between all possible pairs among the 50 runs. 
Panel F: Continuous variables (document-level) 
Documents with identical output (%): The percentage of documents in a sample that have the same output from a 
model across the 50 runs, e.g., same length for summarization tasks and same amount for point estimates of future 
earnings.  
Document-wise MARD (%): The Mean Absolute Relative Difference for a document calculated across all possible 
run pairs. The calculation uses the formula |A -B|/((|A|+|B|)/2)*100 for each run pair, where A and B are the values 
produced by different runs. These pair-wise differences are then averaged to give the document's MARD percentage.  
Mean document-wise MARD (%): The average document-wise MARDs across all documents in a sample. 
Panel G: Text output 
Run-pair similarity: Average cosine similarity between two runs, calculated using text embeddings generated by the 
jina-embeddings-v3 model for all corresponding documents across the runs. 
Document-level similarity: Average cosine similarity for a document across all possible run pairs (from the 50 runs), 
calculated using text embeddings generated by the jina-embeddings-v3 model.

Online Appendix A: 
Task-Specific Experimental Design 
This appendix discusses task-specific experimental designs, covering sample selection, model choice, batch 
size, and API calling. Table A1 breaks down tasks by model, showing sample size, runs, batch size, and 
total API calls. Batch size (inputs per API call) is optimized for efficiency and cost. For example, MD&A 
classification with 10,000 sentences uses GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4o-mini with 50 runs and batch size 10, 
yielding 50,000 API calls per model. The study totals approximately 750,000 API calls, generating over 
3.4 million outputs. 
A1 Binary Classification 
We select 10 -K filings from 2018 to December 14, 2024  (the date when we started to process the data) , 
examining corporate disclosures across the pre- and post-LLM eras. We merge Compustat-CRSP firm-year 
observations with 10-K filings, programmatically extract MD&A sections requiring 500+ words, and use 
stratified sampling to select 5,000 unique firms dis tributed evenly across the period. For forward -looking 
statement (FLS) analysis, we tokenize each MD&A into sentences and randomly select two unique 
sentences per MD&A. To ensure that the sample sentences are complete sentences and are not headings, 
we require each sentence to have at least 100 characters and end with a period. This process results in a 
final sample of 10,000 unique sentences. 
The prompt (Panel A, Figure A1) positions the model as an experienced financial analyst specialized 
in MD&A analysis, providing explicit classification instructions for forward -looking vs. non -forward-
looking statements with standardized JSON output format . For efficiency and cost savings, we batch 10 
sentences per request, separating task instructions (system prompt) from content (user prompt). 
A2 Multi-class Classification 
Our sample period spans from January 3, 2006 to December 7, 2024. The former date marks the first date 
that FOMC statements are available in HTML format on the Federal Reserve website, while the latter date 
represents the date when we began collecting the data. We gather all 162 FOMC statements regarding 
monetary policy during this period. We parse these statements and remove the last paragraph that describes 
the voting results. We then tokenize the plain text into sentences and remove duplicate ones. This process 
yields 1,096 unique sentences, comprising approximately 27,000 words. 
Following Hansen and Kazinnik (2024), we ask our chosen models to classify each sentence into one 
of five categories: “Hawkish,” “Mostly Hawkish,” “Neutral,” “Mostly Dovish,” or “Dovish.” The system 
prompt used for this task is provided in Panel B of Figure A1. In this prompt, we ask the model to play the 
role of an economist who is good at analyzing monetary policy based on FOMC statements. For cost -
effectiveness and computing efficiency, we supply 10 sentences per API call. 
A3 Sentiment Analysis of News Articles 
For this task, we employ the Financial PhraseBank dataset introduced by Malo et al. (2013). This dataset 
comprises sentences extracted from a diverse range of news sources, covering both small and large 
companies across various industries. This dataset was meticulously annotated by a group of 16 experts with 
in-depth knowledge of finance. These  annotators were tasked with assessing each sentence from the 
perspective of an investor, determining whether the news conveyed would have a positive, negative, or 
neutral impact on the corresponding company's stock price. To ensure reliability of annotati ons, each 
sentence was evaluated by 5 to 8 annotators, resulting in the creation of four distinct groups based on the 
level of agreement among the annotators. The agreement levels are 50%, 66%, 75%, and 100%, with the 
majority labels assigned accordingly. We use the full dataset, which consists of 4,838 sentences.


[Page 44]
43 
 
The system prompt used for this task is provided in Panel A of Figure A2. In this prompt, we ask the 
model to assume the role of an experienced financial analyst and classify each sentence as positive, 
negative, or neutral. For cost-effectiveness and computing efficiency, we similarly supply 10 sentences per 
API call. 
A4 Sentiment Analysis of MD&As 
For this task, we generate the sentences from MD&As following the same procedures as those for binary 
classification of forward-looking statements, as detailed in Section A1. The prompt is provided in Panel B 
of Figure A2. We similarly ask the model to play the role of an experienced financial analyst, and we use a 
batch size of 10 sentences to reduce the number of API calls. 
A5 Sentiment Analysis of Earnings Calls 
Our sample includes quarterly earnings call transcripts from 2018 to June 30, 2024, collected from 
SeekingAlpha and matched with Compustat -CRSP data. We require transcripts to have presentation and 
Q&A sections with at least 1,000 characters each and minimum three Q&A questions. For efficiency and 
cost, we subsample 1,000 transcripts using stratified sampling to ensure industry representation (two-digit 
SIC codes) and even yearly distribution, requiring at least two transcripts per industry.  
For sentiment analysis, we randomly select two paragraphs from each transcript's presentation section 
(excluding boilerplate forward -looking statements), requiring 200+ characters per paragraph (relaxed to 
100+ when necessary), yielding 2,000 paragraphs. W e apply identical requirements to executives' Q&A 
responses for another 2,000 paragraphs. The prompts (Panels C and D, Figure A2) position the model as an 
experienced financial analyst, processing data in 10-paragraph batches. 
A6 Summarization of MD&As 
We collect 10-Ks filed between 2018 and 2024 from the Compustat-CRSP intersection, extracting MD&A 
sections with minimum 500 words, as with the binary classification task (Section A1). For computational 
efficiency and economic feasibility, we construct a stratified random sample of 1,000 MD&As using two-
dimensional stratification: selecting one MD&A per unique company to avoid firm -level clustering, and 
balancing across filing years and market value quartiles, given firm size’s association with disclosure 
complexity (Li 2008, Loughran and McDonald 2014) . To accommodate context window constraints, we 
exclude MD&As exceeding 50,000 words (15 cases from more than 25,000 initial MD&As). 
The prompt (Panel A, Figure A3) positions the model as an assistant to a financial analyst following a 
public company, tasked with summarizing the MD&A section. This framing encourages prioritization of 
key insights and metrics valuable to investors and analysts. We include one MD&A per API call to ensure 
inputs remain within context window limits. 
A7 Summarization of Earnings Call Presentations 
We generate a sample of 1,000 earnings call transcripts using the same method described in Section A5. 
We then extract the presentation sections of the earnings call transcripts for summarization. We choose the 
presentation sections because they tend to be lengthy and would benefit from summarization. This approach 
mimics practical applications of using an LLM to aid decision making. The prompt is provided in Panel B 
of Figure A3. We send one presentation for each API call to ensure that the context window is not exceeded.  
A8 Text Generation of Answering Earnings Call Questions 
We generate 1,000 transcripts (as in Section A5) and randomly select two analyst questions from each Q&A 
section. Following Bai et al. (2023), we provide context including a summary of the presentation section 
and previous Q&As up to the focal question. The prompt (Figure A4) instructs the model to role-play as a

questions, the model uses only the presentation summary.  
Summaries are generated separately using GPT-4o-mini with a modified version of the Panel B (Figure

summaries once and reuse them across all runs to ensure identical inputs. 
A9 Prediction of Future Earnings 
Our sample starts with the Compustat-CRSP-EDGAR intersection for fiscal years ending January 1, 2023 
to October 30, 2024 (the latest Compustat date when data processing began). We require positive sales, 
assets, and book equity, excluding financial and utility firms due to different statement formats.  To avoid 
lookahead bias (Dong et al. 2024, Levy 2024) , we focus on firms with earnings announcements after 
November 1, 2023, given two models' October 2023 knowledge cutoff. We require firms to have financial 
data for the prediction year and three prior years. 
We randomly select 1,000 unique companies across market value quartiles, balanced with 50% earnings 
increases and 50% decreases. Following Li et al. (2024), we construct condensed balance sheets and 
financial statements for three years (t, t -1, t-2) as in annual reports, asking models to predict net income 
before extraordinary items of year t+1. 
We provide MD&A summaries generated using a modified Panel A (Figure A3) prompt: “You are the 
assistant to an experienced financial analyst with expertise in predicting future earnings based on company 
disclosures. Your task is to summarize the Management’s Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) section from 
a 10-K filing. The financial analyst will use this summary along with comparative financial statements to 
forecast the company's earnings for the next period.” 
We ask the models to predict both directional change and point estimates (prompt in Figure A5). 
Statements are formatted in markdown for easy parsing (e.g., “| Net sales | 1,990.600 | 1,817.100 | 1,651.400

mimicking human forecasters' approach.

Detailed Consistency Results 
This appendix discusses the detailed consistency results for each task.  
B1 Binary Classification of Forward-looking Statements 
Table B1 presents consistency analysis results for the binary classification of forward -looking statements 
from MD&As, encompassing 10,000 sentences each classified 50 times by GPT -3.5-turbo and GPT -4o-
mini. 
Both models demonstrate exceptionally high consistency. Overall inter-rater agreement metrics (Fleiss’ 
Kappa and Krippendorff's Alpha) yield 0.93 for GPT-3.5-turbo and 0.97 for GPT-4o-mini (Panel A), well 
above the 0.8 threshold for high consistency (Krippendorff 2004). At the run level, mean Cohen's Kappa 
scores across all 1,225 possible run pairs align with these overall metrics. GPT-3.5-turbo achieves 98.07% 
run-pair agreement while GPT -4o-mini reaches 99.00%, a statistically significant difference of 0.93 
percentage points (p < 0.01). 
Document-level analysis reveals perfect agreement rates (identical classification across all 50 runs) of 
92.17% for GPT -3.5-turbo and 96.18% for GPT -4o-mini, a 4.01 percentage point difference. Mean 
document-wise agreement (98.07% vs. 99.00%) and majority class strength (98.67% vs. 99.30%) metrics 
confirm GPT-4o-mini's superior performance (p < 0.01). Mean classification uncertainty remains low for 
both models (0.38 for GPT-3.5-turbo, 0.40 for GPT-4o-mini). 
Both models show higher consistency identifying non -forward-looking statements (98 -99%) than 
forward-looking ones (94 -97%). GPT -4o-mini identifies significantly more forward -looking statements 
(24.30%) than GPT -3.5-turbo (17.76%), likely reflecting improve d detection of subtle forward -looking 
language. 
Panel B shows that GPT-3.5-turbo's Kappa scores average 0.93 (SD=0.01, range: 0.90-0.95) with mean 
run-pair agreement at 98.07% (SD=0.20%, IQR: 97.98% -98.19%). GPT -4o-mini shows superior 
performance with mean Kappa of 0.97 (SD=0.00) and run -pair agreement of 99.00% (SD=0.09 %, IQR: 
98.93%-99.06%). Panel C confirms that while GPT-3.5-turbo's mean document-wise agreement is 98.07% 
with 7.83% of documents showing any disagreement, GPT-4o-mini achieves 99.00% agreement with only 
3.82% of documents receiving different classifications across runs.  
Both models demonstrate remarkably high consistency for financial text analysis, with GPT -4o-mini 
showing statistically significant improvements across most metrics.  
B2 Multi-class Classification of FOMC Statements 
Table B2 presents consistency analysis results for the multi -class classification of FOMC statements, 
examining 1,096 sentences classified 50 times by GPT -3.5-turbo and GPT -4o-mini into five categories

B4 Summarization 
B4.1 Semantic Similarity 
We use GPT -4o-mini to summarize MD&As from 10 -Ks and presentation sections of earnings call 
transcripts. Since summarization aims to provide condensed versions that capture essential content, we 
assess consistency using cosine similarity based on context-aware embeddings from "jina-embeddings-v3." 
Table B4 presents our semantic similarity analysis. GPT -4o-mini demonstrates remarkably high 
consistency, with mean similarity scores of 0.98 for both MD&A sections and earnings call presentations 
across run-pair and document-level measurements. The run-pair similarity (measuring consistency across 
1,225 pairs of runs, each with 1,000 documents) shows minimal variation (standard deviation of 0.00), with 
minimum scores of 0.97 for both text types. 
At the document level, we observe slightly more variation (standard deviations of 0.01 for MD&As 
and 0.02 for call presentations). Minimum similarity scores are 0.90 for MD&As and 0.88 for call 
presentations, still representing strong semantic consistency. These results indicate GPT-4o-mini produces 
highly consistent summaries for complex financial texts. 
B4.2 Length of Summaries 
We assess consistency in summary word count, as prior studies use summary length to evaluate disclosure 
practices, with short summaries of long texts indicating bloated disclosure (Kim et al. 2024a). 
Table B5 shows moderate consistency for MD&As (ICC2 of 0.66, mean Spearman correlation of 0.64), 
although concordance correlation is lower (0.43), indicating systematic differences between runs. The run-
pair Mean Absolute Relative Differences (MARD) of 8.51% quantifies this variation. Earnings call 
presentations show higher consistency (ICC2 of 0.75, mean Spearman correlation of 0.76, run-pair MARD 
of 8.37%). 
At the document level, virtually no MD&A documents (0.00%) receive identical word counts across 
runs, while 0.10% of earnings call presentations maintain identical lengths. Document-wise MARD values 
have notably higher standard deviations (3.38% for MD&As,  4.34% for earnings calls), indicating 
substantial variation across individual documents. While rank ordering remains stable, exact word counts 
vary moderately, suggesting that the models maintain similar judgments about relative information density 
but exhibit flexibility in conciseness.
