Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in financial research
workflows, where their role is evolving from single-model assistance for human
analysts toward autonomous collaboration among multiple agents. Yet real-world
deployments still expose factual errors, numerical inconsistencies, and shallow
analysis, which can distort assessments of corporate fundamentals and trigger
severe economic losses. While existing benchmarks have begun to evaluate such
failures, they score all aspects of the generated analysis in one pass, failing to dis-
tinguish whether a model fails at foundational stages like auditing and correction,
or underperforms at generating research-grade insights. Consequently, it obscures
capability bottlenecks and the specialized strengths essential for multi-agent role
assignment. To address these gaps, we introduce FinReasoning, a hierarchical
benchmark that decomposes the core capabilities of financial research into semantic
consistency, data alignment, and deep insight. We further propose a fine-grained
evaluation framework that strengthens hallucination-correction assessment and
incorporates a 12-indicator rubric for core analytical skills. FinReasoning re-
veals clear capability stratification across model types. Closed-source models
(like Doubao-Seed-1.8) perform strongly overall and are better suited for core
reasoning agents in multi-agent financial systems; open-source general models
(like Qwen3-235B) show clear capability divergence and consistently underper-
form on Semantic Consistency, making them less suited for quality-sensitive
generation tasks; financial-domain models (like Fin-R1) generate moderate in-
sights but lack foundational auditing skills. Our work has already been deployed
in pilot tests across several real-world scenarios. The resource is available at
https://github.com/TongjiFinLab/FinReasoning.

reports Deloitte prepared for Australian and Canadian authorities were found to contain fabricated
references and incorrect citations linked to generative-AI hallucinations [11]. In financial research,
where numerical precision and temporal consistency are essential, such failures are particularly
concerning. These errors can distort views of corporate fundamentals, affecting investment decisions
and triggering severe economic losses. This raises a deeper challenge beyond error detection:
evaluating whether models can sustain reliable reasoning throughout the entire analytical process.
Many existing benchmarks typically treat hallucination as a binary outcome, without assessing a
model’s ability to repair and maintain overall coherence after detection [8, 6, 12]. Benchmarks closer
to financial report generation mainly rely on aggregate accuracy and lack independent evaluation of
key abilities like structured exposition, failing to expose analytical bottlenecks [13, 14, 9].
Financial reasoning is not a single, unified skill, but a structured process composed of multiple inter-
dependent stages. In professional practice, analysts are evaluated across staged competencies—from
ensuring semantic and factual correctness, to grounding intermediate steps in structured data, and
ultimately to synthesizing evidence into high-level analytical judgments. As a result, capabilities are
rarely uniform across levels: both human analysts and models may perform well in certain stages
while remaining limited in others. When these heterogeneous abilities are collapsed into a single
aggregate metric, critical differences in capability profiles are obscured, making it difficult to diagnose
reasoning failures or support downstream system design. Therefore, evaluating financial reasoning
requires a hierarchical framework that can disentangle stage-specific competencies.
To address these gaps, we introduce FinReasoning, an open evaluation resource and benchmark to
evaluate three core capabilities underlying financial research workflows. Figure 1 illustrates the
distribution of tasks, which remains relatively balanced across tracks. Semantic Consistency inspects
whether a model can maintain terminological precision, factual correctness, and reasoning continuity
in long-form financial text. It evaluates the model’s error diagnosis and correction quality through
correctness, reasoning soundness, relevance, and completeness. Data Alignment examines whether
a model can anchor its analysis in structured financial data by performing numerical verification,
indicator computation, and rule-based reasoning. Its execution success rate and field/date selection
accuracy identify where the reasoning pipeline breaks. Deep Insight assesses whether a model can
transform verified evidence into research-grade analysis, using an expert rubric with 4 dimensions and
12 indicators to assess different aspects. This benchmark reveals the capability of LLMs at different
financial reasoning stages, and has already been piloted in several real-world applications, including
multi-agent retrieval and analytical systems in banking scenarios. Our contributions are as follows:
• We release FinReasoning as a hierarchical evaluation resource for assessing LLMs’ reliability in
financial report generation, with tasks aligned to analyst workflows across semantic consistency,
data alignment, and deep analytical insight.
• We provide a fine-grained evaluation framework with documented protocols and a 12-indicator ana-
lytical rubric, enhancing hallucination-correction assessment and enabling structured, reproducible
diagnosis of model strengths and failure modes.

2 Related Work
2.1 Reasoning Benchmarks in General Domain
The evaluation paradigm in the general domain is evolving from single accuracy metrics toward
multidimensional reliability validation [15, 16, 17, 18]. Within this landscape, assessing a model’s
reasoning integrity increasingly necessitates a focus on hallucination detection, numerical precision,
and deep logical analysis. In the area of hallucination and consistency, HalluLens [19] establishes
a rigorous classification system distinguishing between context-conflicting and fact-conflicting,
highlighting the primary limitation of current models due to the ambiguity of external knowledge
boundaries. In the field of structured and numerical reasoning, RealHiTBench [ 20] introduces
complex hierarchical tables and reveals the structural blindness of top models when handling non-flat
data, strongly arguing that relying solely on text serialization fails to meet high-precision reasoning
requirements. This finding complements earlier work on modal alignment in TAT-DQA [21] and
StructText [22]. Regarding deep logical analysis, while FOLIO [23] and ProntoQA [24] respectively
evaluate first-order logic and multi-step deductive chains, LogicBench [25] exposes critical failures
in complex negations and parametric interference.
2.2 Financial Benchmarks
In the financial domain, early evaluation efforts focused on fragmented capabilities [26, 27]. TAT-
QA [16] and FinQA [28] evaluate if models can perform numerical reasoning over hybrid financial
data. To better emulate the complexity of real-world scenarios, recent benchmarks incorporate long
financial disclosures and hallucination detection. SEC-QA [ 13] and SECQUE [14] leverage U.S.
Securities and Exchange Commission filings to test insight generation and risk assessment, while
FinAuditing [29] performs structure-aware logical validation across documents based on international
accounting and disclosure principles. Meanwhile, FAITH [ 12] targets intrinsic hallucinations in
financial tables, and FinTrust [8] establishes a framework for evaluating fiduciary duty and alignment.
Despite these advances, existing benchmarks still focus on isolated aspects of the financial workflow,
failing to provide a unified and fine-grained evaluation of these capabilities within a coherent
analytical framework. Although FinMaster [30] introduces a workflow-oriented setting grounded
in real investment analysis, it prioritizes broad task coverage and offers only limited evaluation of
reliability, traceability, and consistency in complex multi-step reasoning. As a result, there remains
a gap for a hierarchical evaluation that synthesizes financial logic with rigorous staged reliability
validation. In Table 1, we present a comprehensive comparison of FinReasoning with representative
Financial benchmarks in this area.

3 FinReasoning
3.1 Data Source and Statistics
FinReasoning is built upon real-world financial materials sourced from a leading domestic financial
institution and publicly authorized for use. The prompt templates, code, data, resource usage
instructions, and other related information are all available at our project url. For textual resources,
the dataset aggregates 677 pieces of financial literature, over 10,000 publicly available news reports,
and 901 Chinese financial research reports, covering a broad spectrum of market events, industry
narratives, and analytical writing styles. For structured numerical data, we incorporate A-share market
data from 2023 to 2025, including fundamental company indicators and daily trading information.
FinReasoning contains 4,800 high-quality financial evaluation samples. Table 2 reports the detailed
statistics. Detailed statistics are provided in Appendix B.

3.2 Task Definition and Construction
As shown in Figure 2, FinReasoning decomposes the workflow of research report generation into
three sequential capability modules that together reflect the full cognitive process of financial analysts:
reading, verification, reasoning, and writing. We present a detailed data collection process and
benchmark taxonomy in Appendix B.2. DeepSeek-V3 [ 31] is used for QA generation across all
tasks, with all outputs subjected to rigorous expert validation. The generation prompts are provided
in Appendix F.1, and detailed validation procedure is described in Appendix C.1.
Semantic Consistency (SC).SC targets whether a model can maintain coherent semantics in
long-form financial text by identifying and correcting terminology misuse, factual inaccuracies, and
breaks in reasoning chains. This capability aligns with the proofreading stage of analyst workflows,
where one must understand long-text structure, compare information across segments, and accurately
locate potential errors.
In constructing the dataset, we first conduct a pilot study to ground our error taxonomy in real-world
LLM behavior. Specifically, we sample 200 long-form financial texts and prompt five representative
models—DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3-235B, GPT-5, Intern-S1, and Fin-R1—to generate corresponding
QA pairs or continuations. Through expert review, we identify, categorize, and distill recurring
hallucination patterns observed across these outputs, yielding a systematic taxonomy of terminology
misuse, factual inaccuracies, and logical inconsistencies commonly produced by LLMs in financial
contexts. Guided by these empirically derived error categories, we then design nine controllable error-
injection mechanisms that closely mirror the distribution and typology of real model hallucinations.
4


[Page 5]
This design allows us to introduce 1–3 diagnostically meaningful perturbations into each sample
without disrupting overall fluency, thereby simulating authentic LLM behavior under controlled
conditions. The original and perturbed texts are paired with structured annotations specifying error
type, location, and corrected content, all verified through expert review. This approach ensures
our benchmark diagnoses errors that reflect actual model weaknesses, directly aligning with the
proofreading demands of real-world financial auditing scenarios.
Data Alignment (DA).DA focuses on aligning natural-language reasoning with structured financial
data and producing correct outcomes for indicator queries, numerical computations, and rule-based
judgments. It simulates the workflow of research analysts who extract financial indicators from
databases, requiring an accurate understanding of data schemas and the ability to convert analytical
intent into database operations.
In constructing the dataset, we design a rule framework based on a structured database of A-share
market data from 2023 to 2025. For verification tasks, we define 4 categories of numerical-consistency
and text-perturbation rules to check whether natural-language statements match the values in the
database. For calculation tasks, we specify 35 indicator formulas that combine multiple fields
and dates, supporting cross-period comparison and composite metric computation. For rule-based
reasoning tasks, we build 61 executable rules based on established financial analysis practices and
commonly used industry heuristics, enabling professional-grade reasoning in trend assessment, ratio
interpretation, and conditional judgments. All rules are broken into atomic steps and executed through
a unified calculator. During QA generation, we first sample real data and then apply a randomly
selected rule to compute or infer the answer, ensuring that every output is grounded in verifiable data
and explicit logic rather than the LLM’s unreliable numerical guessing.
Deep Insight (DI).This task assesses a model’s ability to generate research-grade analytical
insights grounded in evidence, with logical coherence, causal reasoning, structured perspectives, and
professional judgment. It mirrors the workflow of research analysts, who distill mechanisms, flag
risks, and build clear analytical frameworks from large amounts of information when drafting reports.
In constructing the dataset, we select paragraphs from industry research reports that contain core
events or key mechanisms as evidence, keeping limited redundant context to preserve a realistic
writing environment. Based on these evidence segments, we craft open-ended analytical questions
that prompt the model to reason about the multidimensional impacts of each event. Gold answers are
generated by DeepSeek-V3 in an expert role-playing setup, following professional research-writing
logic: a framing sentence that sets the structure, a three-step causal chain (event → intermediate
mechanism → final impact), and at least one counterbalancing point addressing execution risks,
external constraints, or alternative interpretations. All answers undergo automated and expert review
to ensure every claim is evidence-grounded, and experts further verify the overall analytical quality.

3.3 Benchmark Evaluation
Evaluation MethodAs shown in Table 3, FinReasoning adopts a hybrid evaluation framework
that combines objective metrics with model-based judgments. To mitigate single-model bias, we
employed DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen3-235B as judge models and fused their outputs via grid search
over weighting parameters to maximize alignment with expert annotations. All metrics are normalized
5


[Page 6]
and then averaged to produce a composite score for each track. Detailed evaluation prompts and
scoring rubrics are provided in Appendix F.2.
In SC track, we use sentence-level Precision, Recall, and F1 to evaluate error localization, and employ
BERTScore and SimCSE to measure the semantic alignment of error explanations and corrected text.
To assess hallucination diagnosis and correction quality, we further introduce an LLM-as-a-Judge
mechanism that assigns fine-grained scores to both the explanation and the revision along four
dimensions: correctness, reasoning rigor, relevance, and completeness.
In DA track, we separate evaluation into outcome correctness and process correctness to prevent
cases where a model reaches the right answer through an invalid reasoning path. Answer Accuracy
measures whether the final judgment is correct, while Retrieval Accuracy, computed as the F1 score
over retrieved fields and row identifiers, checks whether the model’s reasoning is grounded in the
correct data source. Success Rate is also necessarily recorded because some LLMs may fail during
the query phase or be unable to return the correct format.
In DI track, we employ an expert-designed rubric through an LLM-as-a-judge to score model-
generated analyses across four dimensions comprising twelve indicators: Justification and causal
depth, including completeness of the causal chain, necessity of causal explanation, and adherence to
professional analytical norms; Factuality and contextualization, examining factual density, computa-
tional rigor, and contextual reasoning; Completeness and comparative analysis, evaluating critical
thinking, comparative perspective, and balance of arguments; Architectural richness and strictness,
assessing framework completeness, logical hierarchy, and expression granularity.
Evaluation ReliabilityWe conduct several robustness checks beyond the main scoring protocol.
Two financial experts independently reviewed 10% of LLM-Judged samples for both DeepSeek-V3
and Qweb3-238B; the resulting inter-expert agreement was κ= 0.79 on SC and κ= 0.76 on DI,
which supports the use of the fused LLM-as-a-Judge protocol as a scalable evaluation mechanism.
Full verification details and fusion strategy are provided in Appendix C.2. Prompt perturbation
experiments in Appendix D.2 show that rephrasing the judge’s instructions has limited impact on
model rankings, with an average Spearman’sρ of 0.95. Bootstrap confidence intervals provided in
Appendix D.4 further indicate that the major performance gaps are statistically stable. We also observe
systematic differences in judge stringency and mild model-family preference in Appendix D.3. The
Pearson correlation coefficients for two LLM judges reach 0.94, so judge-based scores primarily
serve ranking stability and diagnostic comparison rather than calibrated absolute quality estimates.
4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
We evaluate the zero-shot performance of 16 general LLMs and 3 financial LLMs. The closed-source
general models include GPT series (GPT-5, GPT-4o) [32, 33], Gemini-3-pro [34], Claude-sonnet-
4.5 [35], GLM-4.6 [ 36], Qwen3-Max [ 37], Kimi-K2 [ 38] and Doubao-Seed-1.8 [ 39]. The open-
source general models include DeepSeek series (DeepSeek-V3, DeepSeek-R1) [31, 40], Qwen se-
ries (Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-32B, Qwen3-235B) [37], Llama series (Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B) [41]
and Intern-S1 [42]. The open-source financial models includeFin-R1[ 43], TouchstoneGPT [44]
and DianJin-R1-7B [45], all of which are fine-tuned on Qwen2.5 [46]. For open-source LLMs, we
directly utilize their released model weights on Nvidia A100 GPUs during the evaluation. All models
are evaluated under a unified decoding temperature of 0.1. For those who wish to use our evaluation
suite, we provide detailed evaluation cost in Appendix D.1 as reference.

Finding 1: Models can explain errors but struggle to correct them.Across all three tasks, Error
Explanation scores are consistently higher than Correct Content scores, with gaps of 20.2 points for
Terminology, 9.7 for Fact, and 24.3 for Logic. These results indicate that models often rely on surface
clues to describe what is wrong, yet struggle to produce revisions that align with financial-domain
conventions or maintain contextual coherence. The difficulty is most pronounced in Logic, where
correcting an error requires reconstructing an entire reasoning chain rather than adjusting a term or a
number. This suggests that models lack the structured financial reasoning needed to transform error
detection into valid, domain-grounded corrections.
Finding 2: Fact-type errors are easier to detect than Terminology and Logic errors.Fact errors
(Avg. = 52.7) outperform Terminology (40.9) and Logic (40.7) by approximately 12 points. This is
because factual errors, such as incorrect entities or misattributed relationships, create clearer textual
inconsistencies that models can detect from surface patterns. Terminology errors depend on precise
financial vocabulary knowledge, while Logic errors require multi-step causal reasoning.

more often to produce valid outputs as tasks require more steps and cross-field reasoning. However,
the magnitude of degradation differs substantially by model family.
Closed-source models maintain near-perfect format compliance and experience only moderate
accuracy declines, reflecting stronger general reasoning and error-recovery capabilities. This stability
likely stems from their larger model capacity and more robust instruction tuning, which help preserve
structured outputs as task complexity increases. In contrast, financial domain models deteriorate
severely as task complexity rises. For example, DianJin-R1-7B falls from 65.9 (Ver.) to 6.9 (Reason.),
an 89.5% decline, driven largely by its success rate dropping to 16.0%. This indicates that small
financial models lack the structural capacity to maintain coherent output formats when reasoning
chains become long or interdependent.

The DI track evaluates open-ended financial analysis through four dimensions via LLM-as-a-Judge.

results suggest that only a few frontier models can reliably form multi-step causal links, whereas
most models lose coherence once the task moves beyond descriptive summarization. The ability to
integrate events, mechanisms, and impacts appears to underlie the performance gap.

Finding 2: Model scale is helpful but insufficient for deep financial analysis.As shown in

4.3 Discussion
Hierarchical Model Diagnosis.Performance varies sharply across stages. For error detection
and correction in long-text, Doubao-Seed-1.8 and GLM-4.6 show stronger control over factual
accuracy and logical consistency. Tasks requiring numerical verification and multi-step reasoning
are better handled by GPT-5 and Qwen3-Max, reflecting stronger execution over tabular data and
rule-based operations. For higher-level analytical writing abilities, Kimi-K2 and Claude-sonnet-4.5
produce more coherent and well-grounded insights. These patterns indicate that models strong in
high-level analysis do not necessarily maintain stable foundations, while those with strong semantic
reliability may not excel in structured reasoning. Across model families, closed-source models are
more balanced, open-source models show larger variance, and financial fine-tuned models lag in
foundational stages despite moderate high-level performance. This suggests that model capabilities
should be matched to stage-specific roles in downstream systems.
Directions for Improving Financial Fine-Tuned Models.Across all three tracks, financial-domain
models lag behind closed-source and general open-source models of similar scale, with average
gaps of 26.4 points on Semantic Consistency and 29.7 on Data Alignment. Even on Deep Insight,
they remain 13.34 points behind. For example, the 7B financial models Fin-R1 (SC: 22.8) and
DianJin-R1-7B (SC: 20.7) score far below Qwen3-8B (SC: 38.7). These patterns indicate that current
financial fine-tuning improves terminology recognition and event-based summarization but does not
develop the reasoning needed to validate, connect, and transform financial information. Because
training signals emphasize surface-level associations rather than structured inference, models learn to
imitate financial language but struggle with systematic, rule-consistent manipulation of data.
Future development should integrate objectives that enhance reliability and structured operations,
such as hallucination correction and supervised practice with database- and API-grounded reasoning.
These capabilities are increasingly necessary as financial institutions adopt agent-based workflows
that require verifiable retrieval, multi-step execution, and consistent outputs under reasoning demands.
Future Work.Our results show that models perform substantially worse on Semantic Consistency
and Data Alignment (average scores of 44.8 and 62.1) than on Deep Insight (72.5). This suggests
that the fundamental bottlenecks for financial LLMs lie in hallucination detection and correction, as
well as in executing structured, database-grounded operations. However, our current evaluation does
not measure how hallucinations propagate into downstream tasks. For instance, whether undetected
inconsistencies in SC ultimately distort higher-level analysis in DI. Designing end-to-end settings to
quantify these effects remains an important direction.
Moreover, Data Alignment is currently evaluated through controlled interactions with a local database,
which prevents destructive operations but also limits the scope of executable queries. Future work
should develop a sandboxed environment that safely allows the full range of database operations,
enabling models to perform realistic retrievals, updates, joins, and multi-step financial computa-
tions. Such environments would better simulate real-world financial workflows and provide a more
comprehensive assessment of LLMs as autonomous financial agents.
5 Conclusion
We introduce FinReasoning, a Chinese benchmark that evaluates the core abilities required in financial
report generation across three dimensions: semantic consistency, data alignment, and deep analytical
insight, which are specifically constructed through hierarchical tasks. To support rigorous assessment,
we design a framework with a unified metric suite combining objective scoring for error localization
9


[Page 10]
and database-grounded operations with LLM-based judging for analytical quality. Experiments on
19 LLMs expose persistent weaknesses in factual stability and multi-step reasoning, and reveal that
models with similar overall performance often exhibit markedly different strengths across different
capabilities. This indicates that financial reasoning is not a monolithic ability but a composition of
unevenly developed competencies. Thus, our hierarchical evaluation not only highlights a direction
for future financial model development, but also serves as a foundation for designing more reliable
and modular multi-agent financial systems in the future.

A Limitations
The substantive contribution of this benchmark lies in its task design, which closely mirrors real-world business
logic. Rather than confining itself to conventional financial question answering or information extraction, the
benchmark deconstructs research report generation into reading, validation, reasoning, and writing stages that
align with the cognitive processes of human analysts, offering strong diagnostic value for industrial applications.
However, FinReasoning still has few limitations:
• LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation still carries the risk of systematic scoring bias. As shown in Section D.3,
DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen3-235B preserve highly consistent model rankings, but their absolute scores
are not identical. This suggests that the robustness of our judge-based evaluation should be interpreted
primarily at the ranking and diagnostic-pattern level rather than as perfectly calibrated absolute scores.
In addition, since part of the benchmark construction relies on LLM-generated reference answers, the
gold responses may retain certain stylistic or structural preferences. We mitigate this risk through expert
validation, fused judging, prompt perturbation tests, and correlation checks with objective metrics, but
future extensions should further incorporate independent human-written references or judge calibration
sets that exclude model families involved in data generation or evaluation.
• Although the benchmark is designed around the workflow of financial research reporting, the three tracks
are evaluated as staged capability modules rather than as a fully end-to-end report-generation pipeline;
therefore, the current setting does not directly measure how an error in semantic consistency or data
alignment propagates into downstream investment analysis.
• Part of the dataset construction relies on LLM-assisted generation followed by expert verification; while
this enables scalable and fine-grained benchmark construction, it may still introduce residual artifacts in
question style, answer structure, or reasoning templates.
Our future work will try to extend FinReasoning toward cross-market and multilingual settings, sandboxed
executable data environments, and end-to-end evaluations that quantify how local factual or numerical failures
affect final research conclusions.
B Detailed Corpus and Task Statistics
In this section, we provide a comprehensive statistical overview of the FinReasoning benchmark to ensure
transparency and facilitate reproducibility. In section B.1, we detail the composition of the textual corpus,
which aggregates professional literature, financial news, and research reports to simulate authentic analyst work
environments. We also present the specifics of the structured numerical database, encompassing extensive
market indicators and historical trading records across the A-share market. In section B.2, we provide a granular
breakdown of the task distribution across the Semantic Consistency, Data Alignment, and Deep Insight tracks.
These statistics illustrate the structural diversity and logical complexity of our evaluation scenarios, highlighting
the benchmark’s capacity to assess multi-level financial reasoning.
B.1 Data Provenance and Collection Pipelines
FinReasoning Evaluation Resource Construction aims to comprehensively simulate real-world financial research
and analysis workflows by integrating high-quality, multi-dimensional domain-specific corpora. All source
materials are obtained from leading domestic professional financial data service platforms and are accompanied
by public authorization for academic research use.
• Textual Resources:We collected 677 professional documents covering financial industry standards and
term definitions, 901 in-depth Chinese research reports authored by licensed analysts, and over 10,000
publicly available financial news articles. Spanning market dynamics and industry trends from 2023 to
2025, these materials provide a rich contextual foundation for assessing model performance in terms of
logical consistency and analytical depth over long texts. From the above documents, we further extracted
authoritative industry rules and term definitions, and combined these with key paragraphs from research
reports that describe core events or economic mechanisms, thereby constructing a set of text segments
grounded in authentic analytical contexts.
• Structured Numerical Data:We integrated comprehensive market data and company fundamental
indicators for the A-share market from 2023 to 2025. This database comprises over 3.5 million records,
including but not limited to daily trading prices, financial statement line items, and various derived financial
indicators, forming the underlying environment for SQL execution and numerical logic validation in the
Data Alignment track.
• Data Preprocessing:We implemented a rigorous preprocessing pipeline. First, through a combination
of automated scripts and manual review, we removed personally identifiable information (PII) from the

[Page 14]
corpora, such as personal contact details and internal employee IDs. Second, we filtered redundant
content from the research reports, eliminating advertisements and duplicate information, and retaining only
narratively valuable segments with evidential value. This approach enhances the focus of the evaluation
tasks while simulating authentic, complex real-world environments.

C Instructions for Manual Examination
In this section, we delineate the manual examination protocols established to safeguard the quality and evaluative
credibility ofFinReasoning. To mitigate potential biases inherent in automated pipelines and establish a
definitive “Gold Standard,” we implemented a quality control system led by two financial experts, each with
5–10 years of professional experience spanning investment analysis and equity research report writing. In
Section C.1, we detail the expert verification process for data construction, encompassing the evidentiary
grounding, logical cross-checking, and manual refinement of the 4,800 evaluation samples. In Section C.2, we
present the expert auditing procedure for theLLM-as-a-Judgecomponent. Through expert blind reviews and
quantitative agreement analysis, we validate the effectiveness and robustness of the automated scoring system.
These manual examination guidelines, executed by domain practitioners with substantial frontline experience,
provide the foundational assurance for the benchmark’s professional rigor.
C.1 Expert Verification Process For Data Construction
To ensure the reliability of FinReasoning in terms of task definition, answer quality, and financial professionalism,
we conducted expert manual verification over all samples constructed in the QA construction stage. TheSemantic
14


[Page 15]
Consistency (SC)track contains 1,800 samples, theData Alignment (DA)track contains 1,800 samples, and
theDeep Insight (DI)track contains 1,200 samples, for a total of 4,800 QA instances. We invited two experts
with experience in financial analysis and data annotation to independently review and cross-revise all samples
under a unified verification protocol, with the goal of ensuring professional rigor, task adjudicability, and the
stability of the gold-standard answers.
In practice, we adopted a five-stage verification pipeline:Review – Judgment – Consensus – Recheck – Revision.
1. Review.The two experts independently examined all samples. For the SC track, they mainly checked whether
the injected errors had clear financial diagnostic significance, whether the overall coherence of the original text
was preserved, and whether the error type labels, error locations, and correction suggestions were accurate.
For the DA track, they focused on whether the fields, date ranges, and sample IDs required by each question
were consistent with the underlying database records, and whether the numerical answers, retrieval paths, and
structured formats conformed to the predefined rules. For the DI track, they examined whether the evidence
fully anchored an analyzable event, whether the question genuinely required open-ended analysis, and whether
the ground-truth answer demonstrated factual grounding, causal reasoning, and critical analysis. The detailed
verification guidelines are provided in the colorbox below.
I. Semantic Consistency:
• Diagnostic Perturbation Verification: Verify whether the 1–3 injected errors carry clear financial diagnostic signifi-
cance while preserving the overall coherence of the text. Perturbations that are overly obvious (e.g., trivial spelling
mistakes) or overly obscure (i.e., even experts cannot reach consensus) should be flagged and removed.
• Annotation Precision Check: Examine whether the classification of error types is accurate, and whether the location
of the erroneous sentence within the long text is identified with character-level precision.
• Validity of Correction Suggestions: Confirm that the “corrected text" removes the error while preserving all
remaining original content word for word, and that its accompanying “Reason" conforms to financial auditing
standards and is logically persuasive.
II. Data Alignment:
• Consistency with Numerical Gold Standards: Based on predefined indicator formulas and financial execution rules,
experts must cross-check the gold-standard answer against the raw records in the underlying A-share database to
verify its uniqueness and precision on an item-by-item basis.
• Logical Verification of Retrieval Path: Verify whether the fields, date ranges, and sample identifiers (IDs) selected
by the model in complex calculations fully match the logic of the question, thereby preventing the model from
arriving at the correct number by chance through an incorrect retrieval path.
• Format Compliance Review: Ensure that all generated data pairs strictly follow the predefined structured format
(e.g., SQL statements or JSON objects) so as to support automated statistics of execution success rate.
III. Deep Insight:
• Factual Grounding in Evidence: Rigorously verify that every argument generated by the model can be traced to
explicit evidence in the provided research report or literature. The model must not introduce external hallucinations
or unsupported speculation.
• Evaluation of Causal Chain Completeness: Using the three-step framework of “event → mechanism → impact",
assess whether the model reveals the underlying economic logic. Responses that fail to establish logical connections
or merely provide surface-level summaries should be marked as low-quality.
• Multi-perspective and Hedging Check: Pay particular attention to whether the model identifies potential execution
risks or countervailing constraints. Samples that exhibit excessive optimism or pessimism without critical reasoning
should be revised or removed.
2. Judgment.Based on the same standardized criteria, the two experts independently assigned each sample a
ValidorInvalidlabel and recorded revision suggestions. AValidlabel indicates that the sample satisfies the
release requirements in terms of task definition, evidential support, gold-standard answer quality, and format
specification. AnInvalidlabel indicates that the sample contains critical defects, such as unnatural error injection,
a non-unique numerical reasoning chain, an overly superficial analytical question, insufficient factual grounding
in the gold answer, or an incomplete reasoning loop.
3. Consensus.For samples on which the two experts disagreed, the research team organized item-by-item
discussion to align the final decision. The discussion focused on issues such as whether an injected error
was diagnostically meaningful without being overly explicit, whether the data retrieval path was unique and
reproducible, and whether the gold answer truly reflected deep analysis rather than surface-level summarization.
This process not only resolved final labels, but also refined the boundary conditions of the verification criteria,
which in turn constrained subsequent data revision.
4. Recheck.All revised samples underwent second-round verification by at least one expert to confirm that the
original issues had been properly resolved without introducing new ambiguities or formatting errors. For the
open-ended analytical questions in the DI track, the re-check stage additionally required verification that the

completeness, to avoid including samples that were formally correct but not substantively judgeable in final set.
5. Revision.Based on the experts’ feedback, we systematically revised the samples, including rewriting question
statements, replacing or expanding evidence, correcting gold-standard answers, updating error labels, adjusting
data retrieval ranges, and unifying output formats.

C.2 Expert Verification Process For LLM-as-a-Judge
The purpose of this stage is to assess whether theLLM-as-a-Judgeoutputs provide reliable evaluations of model
responses. This differs from expert verification during QA construction, which focuses on the quality of the
benchmark data itself. We randomly sampled 20% of theLLM-as-a-Judgeresults for each model. According to
the benchmark design, only the Semantic Consistency (SC) and Deep Insight (DI) tracks rely onLLM-as-a-Judge
evaluation. Therefore, expert review at this stage was restricted to judge outputs from these two tracks. With full
model coverage over all SC and DI questions, this yielded 11,400 reviewed model–judgment cases in total.
The overall workflow followed the same five-stage protocol as in Section C.1, which isReview – Judgment
– Consensus – Recheck – Revision. The verification criteria were redefined for theLLM-as-a-Judgesetting.
Experts were instructed to examine whether the judge outputs were rubric-faithful, evidence-grounded, and
discriminative with respect to answer quality.
Verification Guidelines for Experts.For each sampled case, the experts were provided with the question,
the gold answer, the model answer, the scoring rubric, and the correspondingLLM-as-a-Judgeoutput. They
determined whether the judge output should be labeled asValidorInvalidaccording to the following guidelines.
16


[Page 17]
I. Rubric Fidelity.Experts should examine whether the judge’s assigned scores and explanations are consistent with the
predefined rubric, including the intended scoring dimensions, severity boundaries, and decision logic. A judge output
should be marked as invalid if it assigns obviously inappropriate scores, ignores key rubric requirements, or bases its
judgment on criteria not specified in the rubric.
II. Evidence Grounding.Experts should verify whether the judge’s reasoning is grounded in the actual content of the
gold answer and the model answer. The judge should not introduce unsupported assumptions, hallucinated deficiencies, or
irrelevant evaluation criteria. Its explanation should be traceable to concrete qualities or flaws in the evaluated response,
rather than generic evaluative language.
III. Discriminative Validity.Experts should assess whether the judge can meaningfully distinguish high-quality answers
from low-quality ones. In particular, the judge should reward substantive correctness, grounded reasoning, and rubric-
relevant strengths, while penalizing key omissions, unsupported claims, broken logic, or superficial responses. Outputs
that are excessively lenient or harsh due only to fluency, verbosity, or superficial formatting should be treated as invalid.
IV . Track-Specific Verification for SC.For the SC track, experts should focus on whether the judge correctly evaluates
semantic error diagnosis and correction quality. This includes checking whether the judge appropriately scores the
identification of injected errors, the validity of the error explanation, and the fidelity of the corrected text to the gold-
standard correction. Special attention should be paid to whether the judge fails to penalize missed key errors, false-positive
error claims, incorrect error typing, or unnecessary over-correction.
5. Track-Specific Verification for DI.For the DI track, experts should focus on whether the judge accurately assesses deep
analytical quality according to the corresponding rubric dimensions, such as factual grounding, causal depth, comparative
or critical reasoning, and structural rigor. High scores should only be assigned when the model answer demonstrates
evidence-based financial analysis, coherent causal linkage, and balanced reasoning. Superficially fluent but shallow,
ungrounded, or overly generic responses should not receive inflated scores.
6. Explanation Quality.Experts should verify whether the judge’s explanation is specific, decision-relevant, and aligned
with the assigned score. A valid explanation should identify the key strengths or weaknesses that actually justify the score
under the rubric. Explanations that are vague, templated, or disconnected from the score should lower confidence in the
judge output and may lead to an invalid label.
7. Validity Decision Rule.A judge output is labeled asValidif its score profile and justification are broadly consistent
with the rubric and with expert judgment on the same case. A judge output is labeled asInvalidif it contains major scoring
errors, unsupported reasoning, weak grounding, or systematic bias toward superficial response characteristics.

D.3 Inter-Judge Consistency and Bias Analysis
Figure 5,6 presents a multi-dimensional analysis of systematic differences between two LLM-based judges
(DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen3-235B) in the Semantic Consistency evaluation track and Deep Insight. (A) The
correlation scatter plot with Pearson correlation coefficient reveals the agreement level between the two judges’
overall scoring patterns. (B) The Bland-Altman plot visualizes scoring bias distribution, highlighting which
specific models exhibit the largest discrepancies between the two judges and detecting potential systematic
favoritism. (C) The radar chart illustrates the dimensional focus and stringency profile of both judges. (D) The
paired horizontal bar chart directly compares the absolute scores assigned by each judge to all 19 evaluated
models, revealing model-specific preference profiles and potential self-bias patterns (e.g., whether DeepSeek-V3
as judge exhibits favoritism toward DeepSeek models). Together, these analyses assess the robustness and
identify potential sources of bias in using large language models as evaluation judges.
First, when using DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen3-235B as judge models, both maintain high consistency in overall
model ranking, although their absolute scores are not equivalent. The Pearson correlation coefficients for the
two LLM judges in the SC and DI tracks reach 0.942 and 0.939, respectively. The scatter plot (Figure A) and the
Bland-Altman plot (Figure B) jointly indicate a stable systematic bias between the two judges rather than random
fluctuations. Qwen3-235B exhibits more lenient scoring across both tracks, with average scores consistently
higher than those assigned by DeepSeek-V3.
In the SC track, this discrepancy is particularly evident at the dimensional level. Figure 5.C shows that Qwen3-
235B assigns higher scores across all four dimensions, with the largest difference observed inCorrectness,
followed byCompleteness. This suggests that Qwen imposes relatively weaker constraints on factual precision
and coverage completeness, whereas DeepSeek-V3 is more conservative and more sensitive to fine-grained
inconsistencies. Further model-level comparisons (Figure 5.D) reveal that both judges exhibit a degree of
in-group preference, but DeepSeek-V3 demonstrates a stronger self-model bias: DeepSeek family models receive
notably higher scores when evaluated by DeepSeek-V3, while Qwen’s preference for Qwen family models,
though present, is weaker. The largest discrepancies between the two judges tend to occur for low-to-medium
quality or borderline models, whereas discrepancies are smaller for strong general-purpose models such as
Gemini and GPT-4o, indicating that bias primarily concentrates on critical samples.
The DI track exhibits a similar but not identical pattern. The two judges maintain high consistency in overall
ranking, yet a stable absolute score difference persists, suggesting that the discrepancy primarily stems from
judge “stringency" rather than ranking reversal. Figure 6.D shows that Qwen3-235B is more lenient on most
models, while DeepSeek-V3 assigns lower absolute scores. Synthesizing the results from both SC and DI tracks,
the robustness of LLM judges should be understood as stability at the ranking level rather than consistency in
raw scores. Therefore, for scenarios requiring comparability of absolute scores, judge calibration or ensemble
evaluation should be introduced to mitigate systematic bias and enhance scoring reliability.

F.1 Data Generation Instructions and Prompts
This subsection presents the prompts used for benchmark data construction. The prompts are designed to

instances with explicit supervision signals. Depending on the task setting, the generation prompts are used to
identify salient evidence spans, introduce controlled perturbations, construct structured question–answer pairs,
or produce reference outputs in JSON format.

You are a financial language expert and a text generation model. Your task is to inject
